# Brand Voice Guide

## Purpose
Brand voice is how your company speaks—the personality, values, and perspective that comes through in every piece of communication. Consistent brand voice builds recognition, trust, and emotional connection with your audience. It differentiates you from competitors and makes communications feel intentional rather than generic. This guide ensures everyone on your team (marketing, sales, support, product) communicates with the same personality and tone.

## When to Use
- Building or refreshing company brand identity and communications standards
- Onboarding new team members or agencies who need to communicate on behalf of the brand
- Creating marketing materials, website copy, email campaigns, social media, customer support responses
- Ensuring consistency across teams when multiple people write for the brand
- When audiences perceive your brand as generic, boring, or inconsistent with your intended positioning
- Developing customer-facing documentation, support articles, or product messaging
- **Do NOT use when**: Creating hyper-personalized one-on-one communications (sales emails, support tickets) that should reflect individual relationships rather than brand tone, writing internal company communications with no external audience, or creating deeply personal content where authenticity requires breaking brand voice conventions

## Instructions

### Step 1: Define Core Personality Traits
Establish 3-5 core personality traits that describe how your brand "speaks":
- **Trait framework**: Each trait should have a spectrum (e.g., "professional ↔ casual," "cautious ↔ bold")
- **Trait selection**: Choose traits that differentiate you and reflect company values
  - Examples: "approachable not intimidating," "expert not condescending," "optimistic not naive," "honest not cynical," "innovative not reckless"
  - Test traits: Do they differentiate from competitors? Do they align with company values? Can different team members understand and apply them?

- **Detailed trait definition** (for each personality trait):
  - **What it is**: Short definition (1-2 sentences) of what this trait means
  - **What it's not**: Explicitly define what this trait avoids or rejects
  - **Why it matters**: Connect trait to customer perception or company values
  - **How it shows up**: 2-3 specific examples of this trait in action

**Example trait definitions**:

| Trait | What It Is | What It's Not | Why It Matters | How It Shows Up |
|-------|-----------|---------------|---|---|
| **Approachable** | Makes complex ideas feel accessible and welcomes questions | Dumbed-down or patronizing | Customers should feel comfortable engaging, not intimidated | Using "we" language, admitting uncertainty, asking clarifying questions, explaining jargon |
| **Honest** | Direct about limitations, challenges, and what we don't know | Overpromising or hiding drawbacks | Builds trust; customers respect candor more than perfection | "This feature is in beta," "We don't have that integration yet," "Here's what we don't do well" |
| **Enthusiastic** | Genuinely excited about solving customer problems | Hype-y or false positivity | Motivates customers and reflects passion for mission | Using exclamation points (sparingly), celebrating customer wins, emphasizing possibilities |
| **Practical** | Focused on real-world application and tangible results | Abstract or theoretical | Customers care about what they can *do*, not philosophical ideals | "Save 3 hours per week," "Get ROI in 60 days," "Reduced errors by 40%" |

### Step 2: Create Tone Spectrum for Different Contexts
Tone is how personality changes across different situations; refine personality to fit context:
- **Tone spectrum concept**: Voice stays consistent; tone shifts based on audience, urgency, and context
  - Announcement of exciting feature: Enthusiastic end of spectrum
  - Explaining a price increase: Honest and practical end
  - Responding to frustrated customer: Empathetic and supportive end
  - Introducing yourself to cold prospect: Professional and expert end

- **Context-based tone matrix**:
  | Situation | Audience | Appropriate tone | Voice signature |
  |-----------|----------|------------------|---|
  | Exciting feature announcement | Existing customers, social media | Enthusiastic, celebratory | "We shipped something amazing! Here's what it means for you..." |
  | Explaining drawback or limitation | Prospects evaluating alternatives | Honest, practical, matter-of-fact | "We don't do X, because our focus is Y. Here's why that decision..." |
  | Customer success story | Prospects, industry | Credible, expert, inspiring | "Here's what Acme achieved. Here's how. Here's what changed." |
  | Support response to frustrated customer | Customer in pain | Empathetic, problem-solving, supportive | "I understand this is frustrating. Here's what we'll do." |
  | Breaking bad news (outage, price change) | All customers, transparency | Honest, apologetic, solution-focused | "This happened. Here's why. Here's how we're fixing it." |
  | Thought leadership, market insight | Industry peers, prospects | Expert, authoritative, perspective-sharing | "Here's what we're seeing. Here's why it matters. Here's our view." |
  | Blog post (educational) | Broader audience, SEO-focused | Helpful, knowledgeable, accessible | "You might think X. Actually, Y. Here's proof." |
  | Email drip campaign (nurturing) | Cold prospects | Conversational, helpful, light urgency | Friendly yet professional; casual but knowledgeable |

- **Tone adjustments by emotion**:
  - **Celebratory**: Increased exclamation points, emphasis on excitement/wins, active voice
  - **Apologetic**: Acknowledgment of impact, clear ownership, concrete action steps, reduced enthusiasm
  - **Urgent**: Shorter sentences, active voice, clear calls-to-action, directness
  - **Thoughtful**: More nuance, acknowledging complexity, sharing perspective with humility
  - **Empathetic**: Validation ("I understand..."), acknowledgment of emotion, focus on customer benefit

### Step 3: Define Vocabulary (Use & Avoid)
Establish preferred word choices that reinforce brand personality:

**Vocabulary to use** (words/phrases that reflect brand voice):
- **Preferred words**: "We believe..." (opinion-driven), "Actually..." (thought leadership), "Let's..." (collaborative), "You can..." (empowering), "Real" or "really" (authentic), "Team" (culture-focused)
- **Sentence structure**: Active voice ("We shipped X" not "X was shipped"); shorter sentences; conversational contractions ("don't" not "do not")
- **Specific over generic**: "Save 3 hours per week" (not "improve productivity"); "Deploy in 10 minutes" (not "quick to set up"); "Trusted by 10,000 teams" (not "widely used")
- **Numbers & specificity**: Use data points, specific percentages, actual customer counts (credibility)
- **Casual markers** (if brand is approachable): Emojis (used intentionally, not excessively), exclamation points (used for emphasis, not every sentence), "👋" in greeting emails, memes or cultural references (aligned with audience)

**Vocabulary to avoid** (words/phrases that feel generic, corporate, or tone-deaf):
- **Jargon**: "Leverage," "synergy," "circle back," "move the needle," "deep dive," "low-hanging fruit" (unless industry standard)
- **Overused corporate speak**: "World-class," "best-in-class," "cutting-edge," "game-changer," "innovative," "revolutionary" (empty without specifics)
- **Passive voice**: "Mistakes were made," "It was decided," "Results can be achieved" (weak ownership)
- **Vague claims**: "Powerful," "easy," "intuitive" (prove it with specifics or context)
- **Negative or defeated tone**: "Probably won't," "Unfortunately," "Limited options" (unless genuinely honest about limitation)
- **Overly technical**: Terms customers don't use or understand (if talking to non-engineers)
- **Trying-too-hard coolness**: Overuse of slang, memes, or cultural references that feel forced; aging yourself

**Vocabulary by personality**:

If your brand is **Expert**: Use confident language, data-driven claims, specific terminology. Avoid hedging ("I think," "maybe"), casual language, admitting uncertainty.

If your brand is **Approachable**: Use "we" and "you," explain jargon, ask questions, admit what you don't know. Avoid technical speak, talking down to audience, over-explaining.

If your brand is **Honest**: Use direct language, acknowledge limitations, say "no" or "we don't" clearly. Avoid hedging, overpromising, hiding drawbacks with marketing spin.

If your brand is **Practical**: Focus on outcomes and actions, use verbs, quantify benefits. Avoid abstract concepts, theoretical ideas, features without context.

**Vocabulary vocabulary matrix** (what to say instead of avoided words):
| Avoid | Instead say |
|-------|------------|
| "Leverage our platform" | "Use our platform to..." |
| "Cutting-edge technology" | "[Specific feature] lets you..." |
| "Best-in-class solutions" | "[Specific metric/proof of performance]" |
| "Improve productivity" | "Save 3 hours per week" |
| "Easy to use" | "Set up in 10 minutes" / "Learn in 1 hour" |
| "Powerful features" | "[Feature name] helps you [outcome]" |
| "Synergy across teams" | "Teams work better together because..." |
| "Unfortunately limited" | "We don't support X because we focus on Y, which means..." |

### Step 4: Develop Real-World Examples
Show, don't tell—examples make brand voice tangible:

**Example set for each communication type**:

**A. Email campaign subject lines**:
Good: "Your team's weekly status in 1 minute" (specific benefit, casual tone)
Bad: "Revolutionary team efficiency solution" (corporate hype, vague)

Good: "We shipped integrations you've been asking for" (acknowledges customer feedback, excitement)
Bad: "New integrations now available" (generic, passive)

**B. Website copy (feature description)**:
Good: "Reduce your team's status meeting time by 50%. No more "what's everyone working on?" meetings—your team's progress is always visible."
Bad: "Innovative collaboration platform enabling seamless team communication and enhanced productivity metrics."

**C. Social media post**:
Good: "One question for teams still in 3 status meetings a week: why? 🤔 (Asking because we shipped something that makes it unnecessary.)" (honest, casual, invites engagement)
Bad: "Disrupting the collaboration space with AI-powered insights." (corporate speak, overpromise)

**D. Support response (customer complaint)**:
Good: "I completely understand your frustration—losing work is painful. Here's exactly what happened, and here's our fix. I'm personally following up on this Tuesday to make sure you're back on track."
Bad: "We apologize for any inconvenience. Our team is working to resolve this issue." (generic, passive, doesn't own problem)

**E. Sales outreach (cold email)**:
Good: "Hi Sarah—saw you just launched a new product. Managing a distributed team launch? I've seen teams like yours waste 8 hours weekly in status meetings. Curious if you ever experienced that?" (specific, acknowledges context, conversation starter)
Bad: "We help teams improve collaboration and drive productivity. Would you be interested in learning more?" (generic, asks for meeting without establishing relevance)

**F. Product update announcement**:
Good: "We shipped API v2 today. What changed: webhooks (finally), better error messages (actually helpful), GraphQL support (because REST wasn't cutting it). Try it, break it, tell us." (honest about what was missing, specific, invites feedback)
Bad: "Introducing our latest API enhancement, featuring advanced webhook capabilities and extended GraphQL support." (corporate, doesn't acknowledge why it matters)

**G. Blog post opening**:
Good: "You probably think data-driven marketing means obsessing over CTR and conversion rates. You're not wrong. But here's what most companies miss..." (disarms assumptions, sets up perspective shift)
Bad: "In today's digital landscape, marketers must understand the importance of data-driven decision-making to achieve success." (generic, corporate opener)

### Step 5: Document Brand Voice Guide Document
Create shareable reference for team:

**Minimum brand voice document includes**:
1. **Brand personality statement** (2-3 sentences):
   - What we sound like
   - How we're different from competitors
   - Why it matters to customers

2. **Core personality traits** (3-5 traits with spectrum, definition, what it's not, examples)

3. **Tone spectrum** (context-based guidance for adjusting tone)

4. **Vocabulary** (use/avoid lists; specific examples for your brand)

5. **Real-world examples** (good/bad for each communication type used by your team)

6. **Red lines** (things we never do, ever—be explicit)
   - "We never claim something we can't prove"
   - "We never talk down to customers"
   - "We never hide limitations in marketing spin"
   - "We never pretend to be something we're not"

7. **Frequently asked questions**:
   - "Should we use emojis in email?" → (Answer contextually)
   - "Can we use exclamation points?" → (Answer with guidance)
   - "What if I'm not sure what tone to use?" → (Provide decision process)

### Step 6: Communicate & Train
Roll out brand voice guide to team:

**Rollout process**:
1. **Announcement**: Share guide with team; explain why consistency matters; emphasize this is a tool to help, not restrict
2. **Training session**: Walk through guide; show examples; answer questions (especially answer "how do I apply this to my specific work?")
3. **Anchoring**: Have someone practice writing something (website copy, email, support response) together, using guide
4. **Implementation**: Start using in new content; gradually update existing assets
5. **Feedback loop**: Team submits unclear examples for clarification; add FAQ as questions come up

**Reinforcement**:
- Reference guide in feedback: "This reads too corporate; let's make it more approachable"
- Include brand voice in content review checklist: "Does this sound like us?"
- Share examples of great brand voice in your company (blog posts, emails, support responses); celebrate consistency
- Monthly: Review new content written and identify brand voice opportunities

### Step 7: Refine Over Time
Brand voice evolves as company and market change:

**Quarterly review** (ask):
- Are we still living up to our brand personality traits?
- Has market perception of our brand shifted? Does our voice reflect that?
- What communication worked best—did it reflect brand voice?
- What communication fell flat—was it because it violated brand voice?
- Are new team members understanding voice quickly, or is guidance unclear?
- Do examples still feel current, or are they aging out?

**Annual refresh**:
- Update examples with recent, excellent communications
- Adjust vocabulary if certain words have become cliché
- Refine tone guidance based on what's worked
- Assess if personality traits still differentiate or if competitors have copied

## Quality Gates

1. **Personality clarity**: Core traits are distinct and differentiated (not generic); each trait has clear definition and examples; team can articulate personality without reading guide
2. **Vocabulary alignment**: Preferred vocabulary reinforces personality traits; avoided vocabulary conflicts with brand voice; include specific, actionable alternatives for avoided words
3. **Example authenticity**: Examples feel like real communications from company (not manufactured); good examples could actually be published; bad examples represent common mistakes or wrong-direction tone
4. **Tone guidance usefulness**: Tone spectrum addresses actual situations team encounters; guidance is specific to context, not generic
5. **Accessibility**: Guide is written in plain language (not meta-language about writing); team of varying writing skill levels can understand and apply it
6. **Differentiation**: Voice guide differentiates from competitor voice in meaningful ways (not just minor tweaks)
7. **Actionability**: New team member or external agency could read guide and produce on-brand content without asking clarifying questions

## Examples

### Good Example: D2C Fitness Brand

**Brand personality**: "We're direct, optimistic, and focused on real results. We don't pretend fitness is easy or always fun—but we prove it works and celebrate every win. Think knowledgeable friend who will tell you the truth, not a gym bro selling hype."

**Core traits**:
1. **Honest**: Direct about what works and what's hard; admit what we don't know; no false positivity. Not pessimistic or defeatist.
2. **Celebratory**: Genuine excitement about progress, wins, and possibilities; emphasis on what's possible. Not patronizing or over-the-top.
3. **Practical**: Focused on real-world application; "here's what to do"; specific metrics and time commitments. Not abstract or theoretical.

**Vocabulary**:
- Use: "Your body will change in 8 weeks" (specific), "This sucks, and here's why it's worth it" (honest), "You've got this" (supportive), "Let's go" (action-oriented)
- Avoid: "Shred your abs," "Get shredded," "Beast mode," "No pain no gain" (gym cliché), "Sculpt your body" (vague)

**Examples**:
- Email subject good: "8 weeks. Real results. Here's the truth about what to expect."
- Email subject bad: "Reveal your best body in 30 days"
- Website copy good: "This program is 4 workouts per week, 45 minutes each. You'll gain strength in week 1. By week 8, you'll notice difference in how clothes fit. Here's what happens between weeks 2-7."
- Website copy bad: "Transform your body with our revolutionary fitness system"

### Bad Example: Generic B2B SaaS

**Brand personality**: "We're professional. We help businesses. We use industry language. We sound like other SaaS companies."

**Vocabulary**: Uses all the corporate jargon ("leverage," "synergy," "solutions," "best-in-class," "innovative")

**Examples**: All sound identical to competitors—"powerful platform," "drive productivity," "enable teams," "streamline workflows"

**Result**: Customers can't distinguish from 10 other competitors; no personality; no differentiation; forgettable

## Common Mistakes

1. **All voice, no substance**: Perfect brand voice applied to empty claims ("Innovatively simplify complexity" sounds great but means nothing). Solution: Brand voice enhances real claims; don't use voice to hide lack of substance.

2. **Personality mismatch with values**: Claiming to be "humble and transparent" but marketing copy is all hyperbole and overstatement. What you say vs. how you act don't align. Solution: Brand voice must reflect actual company values and behavior.

3. **Over-differentiation attempts**: Forcing unique quirks that feel inauthentic ("we're the fun B2B software"—but team isn't actually fun or creative). Audience detects phoniness. Solution: Brand voice should reflect actual company culture and employee perspective.

4. **Ignoring audience expectations**: Being too casual in highly regulated industry (finance, healthcare) where customers expect formality. Or being too formal with consumer audience expecting friendliness. Solution: Personality expressed through tone; adjust tone to meet audience where they are while keeping voice consistent.

5. **Inconsistent application**: Marketing team sounds like one brand; support sounds completely different; sales sounds like third brand. Customers get confused. Solution: Distribute guide; train all teams; include brand voice in content review process.

6. **No examples**: Defining personality traits with long definitions, but no examples of how it actually sounds. Team is confused about how to apply. Solution: Real-world examples matter more than definition; show, don't tell.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **The Exhaustion Trap**: Trying to create 10-page brand guide with exhaustive vocabulary list and rules. Team doesn't read it; sits in folder unused. Solution: Keep guide to 3-5 pages; make it scannable; prioritize examples over theory.

2. **The Inflexibility Pattern**: Brand voice treated as rigid rules that can't flex for context. Team is afraid to adjust tone when situation calls for it. Result: robotic, tone-deaf communications that fail to meet customer emotional need. Solution: Emphasize personality is consistent; tone flexes to context. Give permission to adjust.

3. **The Jargon Substitution Trick**: Avoiding corporate jargon by creating company-specific jargon that's equally confusing. "Synergy" becomes "team harmonization" (same thing, different word). Solution: Avoid jargon entirely; use words customers actually use.

4. **The Personality-as-Gimmick Pattern**: Personality reduced to superficial traits ("we use emojis," "we reference memes") rather than actual voice that reflects values and perspective. When gimmicks become dated, brand voice feels inauthentic. Solution: Personality is about perspective, values, worldview—not tactical quirks.

5. **The Competitive Comparison Trap**: Defining brand voice purely by what competitors aren't ("we're not boring like competitor X"). When competitors change, your identity shifts. Solution: Define voice based on who you are and what customers need, not reaction to competitors.

6. **The Set-It-and-Forget-It Pattern**: Creating brand guide, distributing once, then not maintaining or evolving it. Guide gets outdated; team ignores; new people never see it. Solution: Quarterly review; annual refresh; continuous reinforcement in content review process.
