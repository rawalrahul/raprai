# Presentation Coach

## Purpose
Most presentation failures aren't about content—they're about delivery. Speakers who are under-prepared, haven't practiced out loud, or aren't ready for questions come across as unconvincing even with excellent slides. This skill transforms a deck into a detailed delivery roadmap that builds speaker confidence and audience engagement through intentional pacing, emphasis, natural transitions, and Q&A anticipation.

A coached presentation sounds conversational, not read; lands key messages; holds attention; and handles objections smoothly.

## When to Use
- Major presentations (board decks, customer pitches, all-hands, conference talks)
- High-stakes moments where delivery quality impacts perception
- Presentations you're giving for the first time
- Content that's complex, controversial, or requires buy-in
- Situations where you feel nervous or under-prepared
- **Do NOT use when**: You have fewer than 90 minutes before presentation; slides aren't finalized yet; you have severe anxiety requiring professional help; you're presenting under extreme stress/illness

## Instructions

### Step 1: Audit Your Slide Deck & Story
Before writing speaking notes, review the deck itself:
- Does each slide have a single core idea? (If you're explaining multiple things, split the slide.)
- Are there numbers/data points that need callout? (Highlight or circle on the slide.)
- What questions will this slide raise in the audience's mind? (You'll answer those in your notes.)
- Are transitions clear? (Between slides and between topics.)
- Is the sequence logical? (Audience should follow without effort.)
- Does it have a beginning, middle, and end? (Hook, substance, call to action.)

Audit the story:
- **Opening**: Do you hook attention in the first 30 seconds? (If not, add a question, story, or surprising stat.)
- **Body**: Is the core argument clear? What's the one thing you want them to remember?
- **Closing**: What do you want them to do / believe / feel after?

If slides need rebuilding, do that first. Strong speaking notes can't rescue weak content.

### Step 2: Write Conversational Speaking Notes (Not a Script)
Speaking notes are not a script you read word-for-word. They're a detailed outline that covers:
- What you'll say about this slide
- Where to pause for emphasis
- Key points to stress
- Transitions to the next slide
- Where to look at the audience (not the slide)
- Optional: what to do with your hands/body (if you move when nervous)

**Structure for each slide's notes**:
1. **Hook/transition from previous slide** (1-2 sentences, bridges the gap)
2. **Core message** (what does this slide show, in your words)
3. **Key callouts** (the 2-3 details that matter most)
4. **Why it matters** (so what? how does this connect to the ask?)
5. **Pause point** (where you deliberately pause for effect, let it sink in)
6. **Transition out** (how you move to the next slide)

**Example for a slide showing quarterly revenue growth**:
"So we've covered our product strategy. Let's look at what that strategy is delivering. This slide shows our revenue over the last four quarters—and I want you to notice two things. First, the absolute growth: we've gone from $2M to $8M quarterly. But notice the slope of that line in Q3 and Q4. That's not linear—that's accelerating growth. That acceleration tells us we've hit product-market fit. That matters because it means our strategy is working, and it gives us confidence that we can take the Series B risk we're about to discuss."

Note what's happening: context, specific callouts, emphasis on the important number, why it matters, and transition. The speaker could read that naturally without sounding scripted.

### Step 3: Build in Pacing & Emphasis
Not all slides get equal time. Build a rough timeline:
- **Opening slide** (5-10 seconds): You're settling in, audience is settling in.
- **Agenda slide** (15-20 seconds): Quick tour of what's coming.
- **Data-heavy slides** (30-45 seconds each): People need time to absorb numbers.
- **Conceptual slides** (20-30 seconds): Quicker, because they don't have numbers to parse.
- **Transition slides** (10 seconds): You're moving topics.
- **Call-to-action slide** (30-45 seconds): Land it; don't rush.

Mark in your speaking notes where you'll pause—not for silence, but to let a point land. A good pause feels like 3 seconds to the speaker but feels natural to the audience.

Emphasis lives in:
- **Slowing down** for the most important point
- **Pausing before** the ask ("...and that's why we need...") then stating it
- **Repetition**: Say the core idea in different words. "Market fit means customers need this. They're not trying it—they're paying for it. They're sticking with it."
- **Vocal variety**: Not everything in the same cadence. If you're excited, let your voice show it.

### Step 4: Craft Smooth Transitions (Between Slides & Topics)
Transitions are where presentations get clunky. Audiences notice. Smooth transitions sound like:
- **Explicit bridge**: "So we've talked about what we're building. Now let's talk about why it matters—the market opportunity."
- **Question as bridge**: "You might be wondering: how will we actually reach these customers? Great question. Here's our go-to-market strategy."
- **Story as bridge**: "I want to tell you about a customer call last month that brought this to life. Sarah runs marketing at TechCorp and told us..."
- **Contrast**: "We've covered what's working. Let me talk about what's not working—the gap we're here to fill."

Avoid:
- "Next slide" (clunky; they can see you're advancing)
- "So anyway" (signals you lost your place)
- Long pauses while looking for your next note
- Acknowledging you skipped something ("I'll skip this slide")

### Step 5: Anticipate & Prepare for Q&A
Go through your slides and generate the 8-12 questions you'll absolutely get asked. Write short answers (not scripts, but clear points).

**Question categories**:
- **Clarification**: "You said $8M quarterly revenue—is that ARR or just this quarter?" (Have your definition ready.)
- **Challenge**: "Your market size estimate seems high. How did you get to that number?" (Cite your source, show the work.)
- **Risk**: "What if your main competitor copies this feature?" (Have a credible answer; don't dismiss the risk.)
- **Implementation**: "When will you ship this?" or "How long will rollout take?" (Specific timeline.)
- **Trade-off**: "Won't focusing on B2B limit your upside?" (Acknowledge the trade-off, explain the choice.)
- **Data gaps**: Anything you didn't cover that seems important to the audience.

For each anticipated question:
1. Write the likely question
2. Write your core answer in 1-2 sentences
3. Write supporting details you might add if time allows
4. Write what NOT to say (defensive, dismissive, or revealing something you don't want known)

**Example Q&A prep**:
- **Q**: "Your customer acquisition cost is higher than your competitors'. How do you justify that?"
- **A (short)**: "True. We're at $3K CAC vs. their $2.5K. The difference is intentional—we focus on onboarding-heavy sales process that ensures strong product fit. Our churn is 2% monthly; theirs is 4%. We make back the extra $500 in lifetime value within 18 months and keep the customer longer."
- **A (with data)**: [Same + graph of LTV comparison + retention curves]
- **Don't say**: "We're actually working on reducing CAC" (signals you know it's a problem). "Their numbers are probably wrong" (looks defensive).

### Step 6: Practice Out Loud (Multiple Rounds)
This is non-negotiable. Practicing silently or in your head doesn't work. You'll stumble on words you thought you'd say smoothly; you'll realize timing is off; you'll discover you're speaking too fast.

**Round 1: Read-through** (with notes, not polished). This is about getting the words out, noticing clunky phrasing, finding your rhythm.

**Round 2: Timed run** (time yourself against your allocated slot). You'll probably be 20-30% over. Cut ruthlessly. Better to have extra material than to run over.

**Round 3: No notes** (glance at notes, but try to deliver without reading). This is where you find places you forgot, or transitions that don't work.

**Round 4: With someone else** (ideally someone in the audience, or at least someone who'll give feedback). Deliver like it's real. They watch for: Did I understand the message? Did it feel clear? Where did you seem uncertain? Did you make eye contact? Did you emphasize the right things?

**Feedback questions for your practice audience**:
- "What's the one thing you'll remember from this?"
- "What did you have questions about?"
- "Did anything feel rushed or confusing?"
- "How confident did I seem?"
- "If you were deciding whether to buy in, did I convince you?"

### Step 7: Prepare Your Opening (Critical)
The first 30 seconds determine if people are paying attention. Write and practice your opening multiple times.

Strong openings do one of these:
- **Ask a question**: "How many of you have hired someone only to realize three months later they weren't right for the role? [Pause for hands.] That's the problem we're solving."
- **Start with surprising data**: "70% of engineering hires fail in the first year. We've cut that to 8%."
- **Tell a story**: "I got this call at 2 AM. It was our biggest customer. Their entire team had just walked out because of a feature we didn't have."
- **Acknowledge context**: "I know you've had a long day. I promise this will be worth the next 20 minutes."

Practice your opening until you can deliver it without notes, making eye contact. This sets the tone for everything after.

### Step 8: Prepare Your Close (Also Critical)
Your last slide is where people decide what to do. A weak close wastes everything before it.

Strong closes do one of these:
- **Explicit ask**: "I'm asking for your approval to move forward with Series B fundraising. We need an answer by Friday."
- **Clear next step**: "If you're interested in seeing how this works, I'll be in the back. Grab me. We can schedule 20 minutes next week."
- **Memorable summary**: "Three things we've covered: we have product-market fit, we have a path to profitability, we have a team that can execute. Those three things mean we're ready to scale."
- **Story/vision**: "I see a world where this is the default in every company. That's not a five-year vision; that's a two-year vision. And you can be part of building it."

Avoid:
- "So, that's it" (weak close)
- "Any questions?" on your final slide (makes it sound like you're done)
- Introducing new information in the close (too late)
- Apologizing ("I know I went long...")

Write your close, practice it, memorize the first sentence so you land it.

## Output Template

**Presentation Title**: [Title, audience, length, context]

**Core Message**: [One sentence: what's the big idea you want them to remember?]

**Slide-by-Slide Speaking Notes**:
- Slide [#]: [Transition + core message + callouts + why it matters + pause point + transition out]

**Timing Breakdown**: [Total time allocated, time per section, buffer]

**Anticipated Q&A** (8-12 questions):
- Q: [Question]
- A: [Short answer + supporting detail]
- Don't say: [Defensive/revealing statement to avoid]

**Opening (Memorized)**: [First 2-3 sentences word-for-word]

**Close (Memorized)**: [Last 2-3 sentences word-for-word, explicit ask]

**Practice Schedule**: [When you'll do each round, with whom]

## Quality Gates

1. **Slides are audience-focused**: Would someone unfamiliar with this topic understand each slide?
2. **Notes feel natural**: Could you say these words conversationally (not read like a script)?
3. **Pacing is realistic**: Did you time it? Are you within your allocated slot?
4. **Transitions are smooth**: Does each slide connect to the one before it without jarring?
5. **Key messages repeat**: Did the audience hear the most important idea at least twice?
6. **Q&A is prepared**: Could you answer the 8 hardest questions smoothly?
7. **Opening and close are strong**: Would someone stay engaged through the opening? Would they act on the close?
8. **You've practiced out loud**: Have you delivered this at least 3 times aloud, preferably in front of someone?

## Examples

### Good: Opening (Hook + Context)
"Last week, I made a hiring decision that I regretted immediately. I brought in a senior engineer who had a great resume and interviewed well. Six weeks later, they left, and my team was demoralized. That experience cost us $200K in recruiting and onboarding, plus two months of project delays. And we're not alone—this happens in 70% of senior engineering hires. That's why we built this. And I want to show you how we bring that number down to 8%."

Why it works: Personal story creates investment; specific numbers make it real; clear transition to solution.

### Good: Transition (Bridge)
"So we've established the problem is real and expensive. You might be wondering: can we actually solve this at scale? Great question. Here's how our platform handles it..."

Why it works: Acknowledges their skepticism; frames the next section as answering their question.

### Good: Close (Clear Ask)
"Three things I want you to take from this: one, the market is ready—our beta customers are paying. Two, we've proven we can deliver—look at these metrics. Three, we're asking for $2 million to scale distribution. We need a decision by March 15th. I'm asking you to be part of this. Let's grab coffee after if you want to dig deeper."

Why it works: Summarizes key points; explicit ask with timeline; next step.

### Bad: Opening (Too Soft)
"So, today I want to talk about hiring. I think it's important. Everyone hires, right? Let me show you what we've built."

Why it works: No hook; no urgency; no reason to pay attention.

### Bad: Transition (Abrupt)
"Next slide. So this is our go-to-market strategy."

Why it works: No bridge; sounds like you're just advancing slides.

### Bad: Close (Vague Ask)
"So, that's what we do. If you're interested, let me know. Thanks for listening."

Why it works: No clarity on what "interested" means; no urgency; no next step.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Too much text on slides**: Audiences read ahead and stop listening. Slides should be visual anchors, not teleprompters. Detailed notes go in speaker notes, not on the slide.

2. **Speaking to the slide instead of the audience**: You're looking at the slide to read what you said. Practice until you can glance at it, then deliver to the audience.

3. **Rushing because you're nervous**: Slow down. Pauses feel long to you (2-3 seconds) but feel natural to the audience. Use pauses intentionally.

4. **Not timing the practice**: You have no idea if you're over/under until you time it. "I'll just talk faster" doesn't work. Cut slides instead.

5. **Skipping Q&A prep**: You fumble on a hard question, look uncertain, and lose credibility. Prepare for the tough ones.

6. **Over-memorizing**: If you memorize every word, you'll sound robotic. Memorize the opening and close; outline the middle. Conversational delivery is better.

7. **Leaving new information for the close**: "And one more thing..." on your final slide confuses the narrative. Put substance in the body; close with summary and ask.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **The data dump**: Slide full of numbers with no story. "Here are 47 data points." Your job is to interpret, not present raw data. "Notice three things about this data..."

2. **Over-explaining your enthusiasm**: "I'm really excited about this..." followed by mumbled delivery. Let your energy show through your delivery, not your words.

3. **Apologizing for basics**: "I know you probably know this, but..." If it needs to be said, say it clearly. If they already know it, move faster. Don't apologize for your pacing.

4. **Using slides as speaker notes**: If your slide says "Q3 projections show 40% growth," you read it. That's reading a script. Your job is to say it in your words, with emphasis and story.

5. **Forgetting your audience**: You're presenting to engineers and you're using business jargon; or you're presenting to non-technical people and you use acronyms without definition. Calibrate to who's in the room.
