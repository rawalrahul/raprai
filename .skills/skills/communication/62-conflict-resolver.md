# Conflict Resolver

## Purpose
Most conflicts aren't really about stated positions—they're about underlying interests, values, fears, or needs that aren't being articulated. This skill moves from positional bargaining ("I want X, you want Y") to interest-based negotiation ("What matters to you about X? What are you trying to protect?"). The goal is resolution that addresses root causes, not surface agreement that breeds resentment.

Effective conflict resolution requires psychological safety, curiosity over judgment, and a structured approach that validates both parties while reframing the conflict as "us vs. the problem" rather than "you vs. me."

## When to Use
- Two or more parties in active disagreement about resource allocation, process, direction, or behavior
- Tension is affecting collaboration, decision-making, or team dynamics
- Either party feels unheard, dismissed, or undervalued
- Previous attempts to resolve have stalled or escalated
- Both parties are willing to engage (coerced mediation rarely works)
- **Do NOT use when**: One party lacks authority/power to participate fairly; abuse or harassment is involved; parties are in acute crisis; there's active litigation; either party is unwilling to engage; you lack neutrality/credibility with both sides

## Instructions

### Step 1: Pre-Mediation (Separate Conversations)
Before bringing parties together, meet each individually. This serves three purposes:
1. Understand each perspective without the other triggering defensiveness
2. Assess readiness and willingness to resolve
3. Build trust with you as a fair, neutral party

In each pre-mediation conversation:
- Establish confidentiality: "What you share with me won't be used against you."
- Ask for their story: "Walk me through what happened from your perspective."
- Identify their actual concern: "What matters most to you about this situation?"
- Gauge their openness: "Are you interested in finding a solution that works for both of you?"
- Clarify what resolution would look like: "What would need to change for you to feel good about moving forward?"

**Red flags**: Unwillingness to listen to the other side, desire for punishment rather than resolution, or threats of escalation. If either party isn't genuinely open, pause and address that first, or redirect to a higher authority.

### Step 2: Set the Mediation Container
When both parties are present, establish ground rules for psychological safety:
- **One person speaks at a time**: No interrupting, side conversations, or email/chat during discussion.
- **Assume positive intent**: We're not here to prove anyone wrong, but to understand each other.
- **Focus on impact, not intent**: "What I heard you say..." not "You meant to..."
- **Stay in the room**: If emotions rise, we pause, breathe, and continue. We don't walk out.
- **Confidentiality**: What's said here stays here.

State your role clearly: "I'm here to help you both understand each other's perspectives and find a path forward that works. I'm not here to judge who's right or to impose a solution."

### Step 3: Gather Both Perspectives (With Curiosity, Not Judgment)
Ask each party to describe the situation and what led to the conflict. Use active listening:
- Restate what you heard: "So from your view, X happened because Z..."
- Invite clarification: "Say more about that..."
- Ask about impact: "How did that affect you?"
- Look for underlying needs: "What do you need from [other person] to feel respected/heard/valued?"

As they speak, listen for:
- **Positions** (stated demands): "I need full control of X"
- **Interests** (underlying needs): Safety, autonomy, respect, fairness, recognition
- **Emotions** (the real driver): Fear, shame, anger, hurt
- **Values** (what matters): Integrity, excellence, family, growth

Most conflicts live in the gap between positions and interests. Your job is to surface that gap.

### Step 4: Identify and Name Interests (Not Positions)
Once both have been heard, you name what you're observing:
"It sounds like [Person A], you value autonomy and want to feel trusted to make decisions. And [Person B], you want visibility and collaboration before decisions are made. Is that fair?"

This reframe is crucial: it moves from "You're trying to control me / You're being reckless" to "We have different needs around autonomy and visibility." The conflict becomes solvable once framed this way.

Ask clarifying questions:
- "What does respect look like to you in this working relationship?"
- "What are you afraid will happen if you don't get what you need?"
- "What's one thing [other person] could do that would help you trust this?"

### Step 5: Introduce BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement)
Sometimes conflict resolution stalls because one party thinks they'll "win" if the mediation fails. Clarify what happens if you don't reach agreement:
- Escalation to manager (and loss of autonomy in solution)?
- Formal HR process?
- Continued tension and reduced collaboration?
- Project delays?

BATNA helps both parties see that "no deal" is worse than "imperfect agreement." Ask:
"If we can't work this out together, what happens?" Listen to how they describe the alternative. Often parties realize they prefer resolution to escalation.

### Step 6: Brainstorm Options (Collaborative, Not Positional)
Move from "I want X" to "How could we meet both needs?"

Start with brainstorming rules: All ideas on the table, no judgment, wild ideas welcome. The goal is volume, not quality yet.

**Sample options for autonomy + visibility conflict**:
- Weekly syncs to discuss decisions and input before implementation (compromises neither)
- Decision framework: decisions under $X require alignment; above that, autonomy (clarity + autonomy)
- Rotating decision rights: You lead this sprint's architecture, I lead next sprint's (takes turns, builds trust)
- Async decision tracking: Decisions logged in shared Slack thread, 24-hour feedback window (visible + autonomous)

Generate 5-7 options. Many will be hybrid or creative solutions that didn't appear in initial positions.

### Step 7: Evaluate Against Interests & Commit
For each option, ask: "Does this honor your need for X? Your concern about Y?"

Parties often negotiate quality of solution, not binary acceptance. "I can live with that if we also do Z" is progress.

**Look for option that**:
- Addresses both core interests
- Feels fair (even if imperfect)
- Is reversible or reviewable (reduces fear)
- Has clear next steps

Close with explicit commitment:
"So we're agreeing to [option]. Let's document it. When will we check in to see how it's working?"

### Step 8: Formalize & Monitor
Write down what was agreed, including:
- What will change
- Who's responsible for what
- Timeline
- How you'll measure success
- Check-in date

Send it to both parties. Follow up on the check-in date. If it's not working, iterate. Small course corrections beat months of new tension.

## Output Template

**Parties Involved**: [Names, roles, relationship]

**Stated Positions**:
- [Person A]: "I need..."
- [Person B]: "I need..."

**Underlying Interests Identified**:
- [Person A]: [Autonomy / fairness / respect / safety]
- [Person B]: [Recognition / stability / trust / inclusion]

**Root of Conflict**: [Misalignment between interests or unmet needs]

**BATNA**: [What happens if no agreement]

**Creative Options Generated**: [List 5-7 options, including hybrids]

**Agreed Solution**: [Option selected + why it addresses both interests]

**Commitments**: [Who does what, by when]

**Check-in**: [Date, how success is measured]

## Quality Gates

1. **Both heard**: Did each party leave feeling they were genuinely understood?
2. **Interests over positions**: Did you identify WHY they want what they want, not just what they want?
3. **Reframe landed**: Can they articulate the conflict as "us vs. the problem" not "me vs. you"?
4. **Win-win framing**: Does the solution address core needs of both parties, or is it compromise in disguise?
5. **Specificity**: Is the agreement detailed enough that they'll execute similarly in the future?
6. **Ownership**: Did each party contribute to the solution (vs. you imposing it)?
7. **Reversibility**: Is the agreement reviewable or adjustable if it's not working?

## Examples

### Good: Autonomy vs. Visibility Conflict
**Context**: Engineer wants independent decision-making; manager wants input before major decisions.

**Reframe**: "It sounds like [Engineer], you value autonomy and trust. And [Manager], you need visibility to handle risks and dependencies. Those aren't opposites—they're complementary."

**Option generated**: "Architecture decisions impact the team. How about: you design autonomously, we have a 24-hour review window on Slack for input, then you implement. You keep autonomy; I get visibility."

**Why it works**: Preserves autonomy, adds transparency, creates learning opportunity.

### Good: Scope vs. Timeline Conflict
**Context**: Designer wants 6 weeks for quality; Product wants launch in 4 weeks.

**Reframe**: "You both want customer success. [Product], you're focused on market timing. [Designer], you're focused on not shipping debt. What if we phased it?"

**Option**: Launch core flow in 4 weeks (covers market need); design polish and edge cases in weeks 5-6 (reduces debt).

**Why it works**: Delivers speed and quality, staggers work, reduces risk.

### Good: Process Conflict
**Context**: Team members disagree on how decisions are made—some want data-driven, others want collaborative.

**Reframe**: "You all care about good decisions. Let's build a process that's rigorous AND inclusive."

**Option**: Bring data, allow 30-min discussion to stress-test assumptions, then decide. Combines both approaches.

**Why it works**: Honors both values, creates efficiency.

### Bad: Positional Compromise
"You want X, they want Y. Let's split the difference—they get 60% of Y, you get 40% of X." Neither party gets what they need; both resent it.

### Bad: Authority Imposition
"I've decided: here's how it's going to work." Parties comply but harbor resentment. The conflict goes underground.

### Bad: Ignoring Power Dynamics
Mediating as if both parties have equal power when one has authority to override. Power imbalance must be named and addressed.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Assuming the stated problem is the real problem**: Surface conflict (meetings aren't efficient) masks real issue (I don't feel heard). Dig deeper.

2. **Treating conflict as one-sided failure**: "If they'd just listen / communicate better / be flexible..." Framing it as one person's fault prevents collaborative solutions.

3. **Moving to solutions before understanding interests**: Jumping to options before both parties feel genuinely heard. They won't buy in. Slow down.

4. **Splitting the difference instead of reframing**: "You each give up half" creates resentment. Creative options that address both needs land better.

5. **Mediating when you lack neutrality**: If one party doesn't trust you as fair, your mediation will fail. Either rebuild trust or recuse yourself.

6. **Enforcing agreement without follow-up**: Documenting a deal and walking away. Patterns take time to shift. Check in.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Therapist role instead of mediator**: Going deep into emotional trauma instead of focusing on the conflict and path forward. That's therapy, not mediation. "That sounds painful. For now, let's focus on how you two move forward..."

2. **Siding with the "victim"**: If one party seems hurt and the other seems dismissive, it's tempting to advocate for the hurt party. This destroys your neutrality. Stay curious about both.

3. **Assuming similarity means agreement**: "You're both engineers, you should understand each other..." Different values, risk tolerances, and communication styles exist within any demographic.

4. **Forcing agreement**: If parties aren't genuinely willing to find a solution, stop. Forced mediation breeds resentment. "I'm noticing resistance. Do we need to take a break or would a different approach help?"

5. **Mediating power differentials without naming them**: A manager and direct report have inherent power imbalance. Name it: "I know there's a power difference here. I want to make sure you feel safe being honest." Then create safety through confidentiality and non-retaliation clarity.
