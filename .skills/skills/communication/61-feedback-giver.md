# Feedback Giver

## Purpose
Effective feedback accelerates learning and builds psychological safety. This skill delivers feedback that is specific, behaviorally grounded, and calibrated to relationship dynamics. Using the Situation-Behavior-Impact (SBI) framework with relationship-aware language patterns ensures feedback is received, remembered, and acted upon rather than defended against.

## When to Use
- Giving feedback to a direct report, peer, manager, or client
- Performance conversations where behavior change is needed
- Real-time coaching during or after key events
- Feedback in 1-on-1s, team meetings, or written channels
- Positive reinforcement to strengthen good behaviors
- **Do NOT use when**: feedback is meant as punishment; you lack complete context; the recipient is in acute crisis; there's an active HR investigation; you're angry or triggered

## Instructions

### Step 1: Determine Relationship Context & Timing
Feedback landing zone depends on power dynamics. Manager-to-report feedback can be more direct; peer feedback requires more collaboration framing; feedback about your manager needs reframing as "impact on me" or "pattern I've noticed"; client feedback is always delivered as suggestion, not directive.

Choose synchronous (real-time, 1-on-1) for sensitive feedback; asynchronous (email, document) for complex, non-urgent feedback that lets recipient process privately. Avoid feedback in public unless it's celebrating wins.

**Timing rule**: If you're still activated emotionally about the incident, wait 2-4 hours minimum. The goal is feedback to land, not for you to discharge frustration.

### Step 2: Establish Psychological Safety & Intent Statement
Open with genuine positive regard and clear intent. Frame feedback as "I'm bringing this because I respect you and want to help" not "I need to tell you what you're doing wrong."

**For direct reports**: "I want to give you some feedback on X because I see real potential in you and I want to help you grow."

**For peers**: "I've been thinking about our conversation about X, and I wanted to share an observation that might be useful. Are you open to that?"

**For managers**: "I've noticed something that affects my work. Would you be open to a brief conversation about it?"

**For clients**: "I have a suggestion that might help with X. Would it be valuable to explore that?"

### Step 3: Deliver SBI (Situation-Behavior-Impact)
**Situation**: Concrete context. Date, meeting, project, specific people present. Removes ambiguity.
Example: "In Tuesday's planning meeting when we were discussing the Q2 roadmap..."

**Behavior**: Observable action, not interpretation or judgment. Describe what you saw/heard, not character assessment.
- Good: "You interrupted three times and didn't make eye contact when Sarah was presenting."
- Bad: "You were dismissive and rude."

**Impact**: Specific consequence on outcomes, team dynamics, relationships, business results.
- On outcomes: "The context Sarah was providing would have changed our resource estimates."
- On person: "I noticed you seemed frustrated and disengaged the rest of the meeting."
- On relationship: "It made it harder for me to focus on your ideas because I was bracing."
- On culture: "Newer team members might hesitate to speak up in future meetings."

### Step 4: Pause & Invite Response (Two-Way Conversation)
After SBI delivery, pause. Ask for their perspective:
"I want to check in—how does that land?" or "What was going on from your end?"

This isn't rhetorical. Listen for context you missed. Maybe they were covering for anxiety, weren't aware of their tone, or had legitimate reasons. The goal is understanding, not confession. Defensiveness often signals shame; lean into curiosity, not judgment.

### Step 5: Specify Desired Behavior Change (or Appreciation)
Translate impact into concrete future behavior.
- Clear: "Going forward, I'd appreciate if you'd wait until speakers finish before asking questions. It helps me engage more fully with the content."
- Vague: "Be more respectful."

For positive feedback, specify what you want to see more of:
- "That deep technical analysis in your proposal—keep bringing that to design discussions. It prevents us from building things that don't scale."

### Step 6: Offer Support & Close
Feedback without support is complaint. Offer specific help:
"I'm happy to do a prep call before big presentations if that's useful." or "Want to debrief weekly on how this is landing?"

Close by summarizing and affirming:
"So my ask is X. I genuinely believe you can make this shift, and I'm here to help."

### Step 7: Follow-up & Reinforce
Feedback lands through repetition and reinforcement. In future interactions, notice and acknowledge when the behavior improves: "I noticed you asked clarifying questions before pushing back in our last planning session—that made the conversation so much stronger."

If behavior doesn't shift after three clear feedback moments, escalate to manager or have a more serious consequence conversation.

## Output Template

**Context**: [Relationship type, timing, why now]

**SBI Delivery**:
- Situation: [Date, location, who was present, what was happening]
- Behavior: [Observable action, not judgment]
- Impact: [Specific consequences on outcomes/relationships/culture]

**Their Perspective**: [What I need to understand from them]

**Desired Future State**: [Specific behavior change, 1-3 items]

**Support**: [What I'm offering]

**Follow-up Plan**: [When/how we'll check in]

## Quality Gates

1. **Specificity**: Could someone else who wasn't present recreate the exact situation and behavior from your description?
2. **Behavioral**: Is it about action/pattern, not character judgment or personality?
3. **Impact clarity**: Can they understand specifically what changed because of their behavior?
4. **Relationship-calibrated**: Does the tone and directness match the power dynamic?
5. **Actionable**: Could they write down the one thing to do differently in the next situation?
6. **Balanced**: If critical feedback, does it include acknowledgment of intent or context?
7. **Owned**: Did you own your emotional response? "That triggered me" vs. "You made me angry"

## Examples

### Good: Manager to Report (Real-Time, Critical)
"In the standup this morning when Tom was explaining the API blocker, I noticed you looked at Slack three times and didn't engage with his questions. That matters because Tom needed to know the design team was invested in solving this, and he walked away unsure. Going forward, I'd like you to put your phone away and ask one clarifying question during blockers. I know you care about this, but your engagement is what signals that."

### Good: Peer to Peer (Positive, Asynchronous)
"I wanted to flag something I noticed in the client presentation yesterday. When Jessica pushed back on our timeline, you acknowledged her constraint and reframed the proposal instead of defending our original plan. That's exactly the collaborative approach that's been missing. I'd love to see more of that in our negotiations—it shifts how clients respond."

### Good: Report to Manager (Feedback Up, Framed as Impact)
"I want to bring something up because I value our relationship. In our last three 1-on-1s, you've jumped straight to problem-solving without asking what I need from you. It's made me hesitant to bring issues early because I feel a bit steam-rolled. I'd really appreciate a few minutes where you ask 'What would be helpful?' before diving in."

### Bad: Judgment-Based, Vague
"You were rude and dismissive in the meeting. It's unprofessional. You need to be a better team player."

### Bad: Escalates Shame Instead of Growth
"Everyone saw what happened. Your behavior was unacceptable. You should know better."

### Bad: Focused on Intent Instead of Impact
"I know you were trying to be helpful, but your tone sounded critical. You should think about how people perceive you."

## Common Mistakes

1. **Leading with intent instead of impact**: "I know you didn't mean to, but..." minimizes their impact. Start with impact, then invite their intent. Their intent doesn't change the impact, but it informs whether this is a pattern or one-off.

2. **Mixing feedback topics**: "You interrupt AND you miss deadlines AND you don't update Jira." Pick one behavior. Multiple topics dilute focus and trigger defensiveness.

3. **Feedback as gotcha**: Saving up feedback to use as ammunition in a performance review rather than giving it in real-time. This signals you were letting them fail rather than helping them grow.

4. **Softening so much it disappears**: "You're generally great, but in one small thing in one meeting, you might have possibly..." The receiver won't hear it. Be kind AND clear.

5. **Attributing behavior to character**: "You're not a listener" vs. "I noticed you didn't ask questions in this conversation." One closes doors; one opens them.

6. **Feedback without follow-up**: Delivering feedback and never checking if it landed, improving, or persisting. Feedback without accountability is venting.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **"Feedback sandwich" with invisible meat**: Praise, tiny critique buried in neutral language, more praise. Receivers often miss the actual feedback. Use SBI directly; don't hide it.

2. **Feedback as performance theater**: Giving feedback in a way that makes you look good to observers rather than actually helping the person. If an audience is watching, move the conversation.

3. **Retrospective diagnosis**: "You've always been like this" or "This is typical of you." Feedback is about a specific moment and choice, not their essence.

4. **Feedback without equity**: Giving feedback about communication style or presence to women and people of color while ignoring the same behavior in others. Audit who receives feedback and for what.

5. **Feedback avoidance**: Never giving critical feedback and then rating someone lower. That's punishment disguised as fairness. Feedback is a gift; withholding it is a choice to let someone fail.
