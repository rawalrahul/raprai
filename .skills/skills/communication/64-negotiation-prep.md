# Negotiation Prep

## Purpose
Most negotiations are won before the conversation starts. Preparation determines outcome more than talking skill. This skill builds a negotiation roadmap that clarifies your true boundaries, identifies the other party's constraints, maps the zone of agreement, and plans your anchor, concession sequence, and walk-away point. The result is confident execution that protects your interests while building a relationship you want to maintain.

## When to Use
- Salary negotiation, equity negotiations, contract discussions
- Major vendor or customer agreements (pricing, terms, scope)
- Job offers, partnership deals, acquisition/investment terms
- Internal negotiation (resource allocation, project scope, timeline)
- Any discussion where money, terms, or commitment is at stake
- **Do NOT use when**: You're in acute anger or desperation; you lack authority to negotiate; the other party is acting in bad faith; you haven't gathered basic information about their constraints

## Instructions

### Step 1: Gather Intelligence on the Other Party
Before you negotiate, understand their situation. Your goal: What do they need? What are they constrained by? What matters to them?

**Where to find intelligence**:
- **Previous conversations**: What did they say about budget, timeline, pain points?
- **Market data**: What do similar roles, contracts, or deals look like? (This anchors your expectations.)
- **Their public statements**: LinkedIn, interviews, earnings calls, company website. What are their priorities?
- **Mutual connections**: Call someone who's negotiated with them before. "What was your experience? What did they care most about?"
- **Their financial health**: Publicly traded? Check recent earnings. Private? Look for signs of strength/stress.
- **Their alternatives**: If they don't agree with you, what do they do? (If they have weak alternatives, you have leverage.)

**What you're trying to learn**:
- What are they optimizing for? (Cost? Speed? Quality? Risk reduction?)
- What's their budget or approval authority?
- What are their hard constraints? (Legal, regulatory, board-approved?)
- What's their deadline?
- Who's the decision-maker, and what do they care about?
- What would happen to them if the deal doesn't happen?

### Step 2: Clarify Your BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement)
BATNA is not "the deal fails." It's the realistic alternative you'll pursue if this deal doesn't work.

If you're negotiating salary, your BATNA is: "I stay in my current role" or "I take another offer" or "I freelance," not "I'm unemployed."

If you're negotiating vendor terms, your BATNA is: "I use Vendor B at [their terms]" or "I build in-house," not "I have no option."

Be honest about your BATNA:
- Is it actually attractive to you? (If your BATNA is weak, you have less leverage.)
- What does it cost you in time, money, or opportunity?
- How long can you sustain it?
- Does the other party know your BATNA? (Sometimes revealing it helps; sometimes it weakens you.)

**Calculate your BATNA value**:
- Salary negotiation: "If I don't take this role, I stay in my current $150K role. So $150K is my BATNA. Anything above that is upside."
- Vendor negotiation: "Vendor B would cost $500K and take 6 months to implement. So that's the cost of my BATNA."

Your BATNA informs your walk-away point (see Step 4).

### Step 3: Map the ZOPA (Zone of Possible Agreement)
ZOPA is the range where both parties can win. Outside the ZOPA, one party walks away.

**Build your map**:
1. **Your ideal**: What do you want in a perfect scenario?
2. **Your acceptable minimum**: Below this, you walk away (your BATNA is better).
3. **Their likely ideal**: What do they probably want?
4. **Their likely acceptable minimum**: Below this, they walk away.

**Example (salary negotiation)**:
- Your ideal: $200K base + $50K bonus + 2% equity
- Your acceptable minimum: $160K base + $20K bonus + 0.5% equity (better than your $150K BATNA)
- Their likely ideal: $140K + $10K bonus + 0.25% equity (save money)
- Their likely acceptable minimum: $155K + $15K bonus + 0.5% equity (they don't want to pay more than peer roles; don't want to re-open search)

**The ZOPA**: $155K–$160K base + $15K–$20K bonus + 0.5% equity

Anything in this zone means both parties prefer the deal to their alternatives. That's where you negotiate.

If there's no overlap, the ZOPA is very small or non-existent. You may need to expand it by finding other variables (see Step 5).

### Step 4: Determine Your Walk-Away Point
This is the line you won't cross. It's informed by your BATNA and your minimums across all variables.

Walk-away points that work:
- "I won't take a role for less than $155K base because my BATNA is $150K and I need the upgrade to be worth the risk."
- "I need 1% equity minimum because [alternative offers have that]."
- "The project timeline can't slip past Q4 because our business case depends on H1 revenue."

Know your walk-aways before you sit down. During negotiation, you'll be tempted to shift them. Knowing them in advance prevents desperation moves.

**How to hold your walk-away without being rigid**:
- You can explore creative solutions that honor your core constraint.
- "I need $155K base OR $140K + 2% equity. Those are equivalent to me."
- "The March deadline is hard. But if you can commit to feature X, I can work with April."
- Flexibility in how you achieve the minimum, rigidity in the minimum itself.

### Step 5: Identify Integrative (Non-Zero-Sum) Variables
Most negotiations treat every variable as zero-sum: my gain is your loss. But often there are variables where different priorities create upside for both.

**Examples of integrative variables**:
- **Payment terms vs. price**: They want lower price; you need fast cash. Solution: they pay higher price with 50% upfront, 50% in 30 days.
- **Salary vs. equity**: They're cost-constrained; you're growth-oriented. Solution: lower salary, higher equity.
- **Scope vs. timeline**: You're under-resourced; they need fast delivery. Solution: reduce scope, hit timeline, phase 2 in next quarter.
- **Price vs. volume**: You're cost-sensitive; they're revenue-hungry. Solution: lower price on high-volume commitment.
- **Terms vs. relationship**: They want favorable terms; you want a long partnership. Solution: favorable terms + multi-year agreement.

Before negotiations, identify 3-5 variables where your priorities might differ. These become your negotiation leverage.

### Step 6: Choose Your Anchor & Justification
The anchor is your opening ask. It disproportionately influences the final outcome. Research shows first mover with a strong anchor wins.

**Rules for anchoring**:
- **Be ambitious but defensible**: $300K for a role that markets at $180K looks ridiculous. $195K (on the high end of market) looks ambitious but reasonable.
- **Have a justification**: "Based on market data for [title/location], salaries range from $175K–$195K. Given my experience in [domain], I'm asking for $195K."
- **Don't anchor too low**: If you say $160K and you're willing to accept $175K, you've just anchored yourself down.
- **Let them respond**: You anchor; you pause. Don't immediately say "but I could do $X." Let them respond to your anchor first.

**Example anchors**:
- **Salary**: "Based on Glassdoor, Levels.fyi, and your stated growth stage, roles like this go for $180K–$210K. I'm asking for $200K."
- **Vendor pricing**: "Market rate for this service is $50K–$80K annually. I'm asking for $75K with a multi-year discount."
- **Equity**: "Market rates for this level are 0.5%–2%. I'm asking for 1.5%."

Anchor on the variable that matters most to you. If salary matters most, anchor there. Let them win on other variables.

### Step 7: Plan Your Concession Sequence
You'll make concessions. Plan them in advance so you:
- Don't give away your most valuable chips first
- Make concessions smaller as negotiation progresses (signals you're near your limit)
- Trade concessions ("If I move on X, I need movement on Y")

**Concession sequence example (salary negotiation)**:
1. **First move** (if they push back on $200K anchor): "$195K is where I land. That's 15% above my current role and reflects the market data I shared."
2. **Second move** (if they push back): "$190K + $25K signing bonus + 1.5% equity. That gets to my number when you factor in the signing bonus and equity upside."
3. **Third move** (if they still push): "$185K + $25K signing bonus + 1.5% equity. This is close to my limit. For me to move further, we'd need to revisit equity or get to a firm promotion date."
4. **Walk-away**: "$155K base is my floor. Below that, my BATNA of staying in my current role is better."

Notice: concessions get smaller (200→195→190→185), and by move 3, you're signaling you're near limits.

### Step 8: Plan Your Concession Trading
Don't give without getting. If they want you to move on salary, ask for movement on equity or title or timeline.

**Examples**:
- "I can move to $185K if you can move to 1.25% equity."
- "I can extend the timeline to April if you can commit to feature X."
- "I can reduce scope if we can increase the budget for the core module."

This signals that concessions have value and prevents them from nibbling at every variable.

### Step 9: Prepare Rapport-Building & Information-Seeking Questions
Negotiation isn't purely transactional. Build relationship and gather information through questions.

**Information-seeking questions**:
- "What does success look like for you in this?"
- "What constraints are you working with on your end?"
- "Is there something about your company's strategy or situation I should understand?"
- "What happened in previous deals like this?"

**Rapport-building questions**:
- "How long have you been in this role?"
- "What drew you to [company]?"
- "How are you thinking about [industry trend]?"

Ask questions early and listen. People who feel heard are more flexible. Also, their answers might reveal opportunities you didn't know about.

### Step 10: Anticipate Objections & Prepare Responses
The other party will push back. Prepare for the hardest objections.

**Likely objection**: "Your ask is above our budget."
**Your response**: "I understand budget is tight. Let me show you the market data. Roles at this level go for [range]. Given [my background], I'm at the high end, but it's market-rate. What would help make this work within your constraints?"

**Likely objection**: "We need to move faster than your timeline."
**Your response**: "I hear the urgency. My concern is [quality/thoroughness]. What if we break this into [phase 1 + 2]? We hit your deadline on phase 1; phase 2 starts immediately."

**Likely objection**: "Your price is too high compared to competitors."
**Your response**: "I understand cost matters. Our price reflects [quality/support/outcomes]. Competitor pricing is lower because [context]. If price is the deciding factor, I can't compete. But if you value [X], the value prop is worth the premium."

For each likely objection, write:
1. The objection (in their words, not a strawman)
2. Your response (validating + clarifying + solving)
3. A question that moves toward agreement

### Step 11: Plan Your Closing Language
When you're near agreement, close clearly. Vague closes lead to renegotiation later.

**Strong closing language**:
- "So we're at: $180K base, $20K bonus, 1% equity, 4-week start date. Does that capture it?"
- "Let me write this down so we're both clear: [terms]. Does this work for you?"
- "I want to confirm I'm understanding correctly: [terms]. Is that right?"

Get verbal commitment, then follow up with written confirmation within 24 hours.

### Step 12: Know When to Walk Away
Sometimes the best outcome is walking away. You walk away when:
- The deal falls below your walk-away point
- The other party is negotiating in bad faith
- The relationship cost exceeds the deal value
- Your BATNA is genuinely better

When you walk away, do it cleanly:
- "I appreciate the offer. It doesn't quite work for me. I'm going to pursue [alternative]. I'm open if things change on your end."
- Don't burn the bridge. Leave space to come back to the table.

## Quality Gates

1. **ZOPA is realistic**: Is there actually overlap, or is this a waste of time?
2. **Your anchor is ambitious but defensible**: Would a reasonable person see it as possible?
3. **Concession sequence is planned**: Can you move without desperation?
4. **You've thought through integrative variables**: Are there creative solutions beyond positional bargaining?
5. **Walk-away is real**: Could you actually walk away if needed?
6. **You've gathered intelligence**: Do you understand their constraints and priorities?
7. **Objections are anticipated**: Could you handle pushback smoothly?
8. **Relationship matters**: Are you building connection or just transacting?

## Examples

### Good: Salary Negotiation Prep
**Intelligence**: Startup in Series B, raised $20M. Role is new, created to support growth. Market rate for role is $170K–$210K. Similar roles at peer companies average $190K. They want to hire in 4 weeks.

**Your BATNA**: Stay in current role at $155K. Could take freelance work at $50K/month if needed.

**ZOPA**: You want $190K–$210K. They likely want $160K–$175K. ZOPA is roughly $175K–$190K.

**Your walk-aways**: "$165K base minimum (good upgrade on BATNA) OR $145K + 1.5% equity (equivalent value). Won't accept <$165K unless equity significant."

**Anchor**: "$200K base. Based on market data ($170K–$210K), role complexity, and my background in [domain], I'm anchoring at the high end."

**Concession sequence**: $200K → $195K → $190K → $185K + $20K bonus → $175K + $25K bonus + 1.25% equity (this is your ZOPA).

**Integrative variables**: "If cash is tight, I can flex on equity. If growth is uncertain, I want clear promotion criteria."

### Good: Vendor Negotiation Prep
**Intelligence**: Vendor is hungry for annual contracts (their board wants recurring revenue). Competitor charges $55K/year. Vendor's list price is $70K/year.

**Your BATNA**: Build in-house over 6 months at $120K total, or use Competitor B at $55K/year. Building in-house is preferred but takes time.

**ZOPA**: You want $40K–$50K/year. Vendor wants $65K–$70K. ZOPA is $50K–$65K.

**Your walk-aways**: "$50K/year OR $55K/year with multi-year discount. Won't pay $65K+ per year."

**Anchor**: "$40K/year. Competitor charges $55K; vendor's switching costs and setup are worth $15K value to you, so $40K is fair."

**Concession sequence**: $40K → $45K → $50K + 2-year commitment → $55K/year on 3-year deal (at $165K total, that's good value).

**Integrative variables**: "Multi-year contract (they want recurring revenue) in exchange for lower annual rate."

### Bad: No Intelligence, Hope-Based Negotiation
"I'll ask for $200K and see what they say. If they push back, I'll take $180K. I don't know what they're working with, but I think that's fair."

This leaves money on the table and shows lack of preparation.

### Bad: Weak Anchor
"I'm asking for $160K, but I'd probably take $155K. I don't want to ask too high."

This just anchors yourself down. You've told them your floor without hearing their opening.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Anchoring too low because you don't want to offend**: You've just negotiated against yourself. Ambitious anchors are expected and respected in negotiation.

2. **Giving away your most valuable concession first**: If salary is most important to you, don't move there first. Move on less important variables first to signal flexibility while protecting what matters.

3. **Not planning your concession sequence**: You end up reactive, moving on every variable, looking desperate.

4. **Treating negotiation as pure conflict**: You're leaving value on the table. Look for integrative variables where you can both win.

5. **Bluffing on your walk-away**: If you say you'll walk away and don't, you've destroyed your credibility. Only use walk-away if you mean it.

6. **Not asking questions**: You're negotiating based on assumptions. Ask about their constraints, priorities, and alternatives. Their answers often reveal creative solutions.

7. **Negotiating without writing it down**: Agreement that lives only in conversation gets renegotiated. Confirm in writing within 24 hours.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Giving without trading**: "Sure, I can move to $185K" without asking what they're moving on. You've signaled you'll keep moving. Trade every concession.

2. **Getting anchored by their opening without pushback**: They say "$140K" and you respond "$180K." You've accepted their anchor as baseline. Counter-anchor: "I'm looking at $200K. Here's why [market data]."

3. **Negotiating variables out of sequence**: Fighting over salary first, then finding out they can't move on equity. Identify all variables first, then prioritize. Ask "What can we flex on?" early.

4. **Making the negotiation personal**: "You're trying to lowball me" or "Your company doesn't value people." This triggers defensiveness. Keep it professional: "The market data shows [X]. Let's see how we bridge it."

5. **Assuming their BATNA is as weak as you think**: You assume they'll never hire anyone else if they don't hire you. But they might. Do the math on their actual alternatives before assuming you have leverage.
