# Onboarding Guide

## Purpose
The first 90 days determine whether a new hire becomes productive, stays engaged, and fits the culture. This skill creates a structured onboarding roadmap that accelerates learning, builds relationships, clarifies expectations, reduces anxiety, and prevents the slow ramp-up that wastes months. A good onboarding guide is personalized (to the person, role, and team), specific (not generic HR tasks), and removes friction so the new person can focus on learning.

## When to Use
- Pre-hire planning (before person accepts offer or starts)
- First day/week planning
- Creating ongoing onboarding materials for future hires in the same role
- Returning from leave or significant role changes
- **Do NOT use when**: Creating generic company onboarding without role-specific context; onboarding is reactive instead of planned

## Instructions

### Step 1: Gather Pre-Hire Information
Before the person's first day, collect context:

**About the person**:
- Background (prior roles, expertise, knowledge gaps)
- Why they joined (mission, team, compensation, growth opportunity)
- What they're worried about or curious about
- Learning style (hands-on, reading, shadowing, discussion)
- Any accommodations or preferences

**About the role**:
- Core responsibilities (not job description; actual work they'll do)
- Success metrics (how you'll measure if they're succeeding at 30, 60, 90 days)
- Team composition (who they'll work with, who's their closest collaborator)
- Key dependencies (who do they need to coordinate with)
- Biggest challenge they'll face (so you can prepare them)

**About the team/company**:
- Team norms (how decisions are made, communication style, meeting culture)
- Company values and how they manifest
- Current priorities and challenges
- Political landscape (key stakeholders, power dynamics, tensions)
- Unwritten rules (what matters in your culture beyond official policies)

### Step 2: Create 30-60-90 Day Milestones
Instead of "learn the codebase," create specific, measurable milestones.

**Day 30 (Onboarding)**:
Success = New person is functional, knows who everyone is, understands the domain.

Example milestones:
- Completed setup (environment, access, tools). Can work independently without help.
- Shadowed 3 key processes end-to-end (customer flow, deployment process, incident response).
- 1-on-1 with each key stakeholder. Understands their role and how you work with them.
- Completed first small task or support ticket. Shipped something (even if tiny).
- Understands team roadmap, current priorities, and strategic context.
- Attended team standup, planning, retro. Familiar with how team works.
- Read documentation on domain, architecture, past decisions. Asks informed questions.
- Identified one thing that's confusing or broken (and asked about it). Shown curiosity.

**Day 60 (Contributing)**:
Success = New person is adding value, knows what good looks like, starting to influence.

Example milestones:
- Completed 2-3 projects or features independently (with code review/feedback). Quality acceptable.
- Participated meaningfully in discussions. Given opinions that are valued.
- Identified process improvement or bug. Drove solution with team support.
- 1-on-1 with manager: discussed fit, asked clarifying questions, got feedback on performance so far.
- Built relationships outside immediate team. Grabbed coffee with 2-3 people.
- Knows the customer/market. Attended customer call or demo.
- Asking fewer clarifying questions; more proactive problem-solving.
- Participated in on-call or incident. Handled production issue with support.

**Day 90 (Independent)**:
Success = New person is independent contributor, embodying culture, seen as team member.

Example milestones:
- Led a project or feature from start to finish. Shipped to customers.
- Made a decision independently that was right. Showed judgment.
- Helped onboard/teach someone else. Passed knowledge forward.
- Attended strategy meeting or planning cycle. Contributing to big-picture thinking.
- Identified and fixed a major inefficiency or technical debt. Owned improvement.
- Gave feedback to manager. Not just receiving feedback; giving it too.
- Felt like "one of us." People include them in team conversations, decisions, social events.
- 90-day check-in with manager: where are we on success criteria? What's next?

Milestones should be **observable and measurable**, not vague ("learns the role").

### Step 3: Build the First Week Checklist
First week is overwhelm-heavy. Remove friction with pre-planning.

**Before they arrive**:
- Equipment ordered and set up (laptop, monitor, seat)
- Accounts created (email, GitHub, Slack, etc.)
- Access provisioned (repos, databases, test environments)
- Documentation prepared (architecture overview, glossary, key processes)
- Team notified; calendar blocked for intro meetings
- Manager has time blocked (no vacation week 1)
- Buddy assigned (someone to answer dumb questions safely)

**Day 1 (Logistics + Welcome)**:
- 30-min welcome from manager (here's what to expect, here's where everything is, what should I know about working with you?)
- IT setup: laptop, accounts, access verified
- Tour (office, kitchen, bathroom, meeting rooms) if in-office
- Quick 1-on-1 with buddy (not manager). Buddy: answers setup questions, shows informal ropes
- Team intro lunch or coffee
- Review role description, org chart, team structure
- Task: Set up development environment (or first system task). Ask for help if stuck.
- End of day: 15-min check-in with manager. How are you feeling? What do you need?

**Day 2-3 (Learning)**:
- Daily standup. Listen. Don't contribute yet.
- Architecture overview from senior engineer (1 hour). Goal: understand the basics, not mastery.
- Customer context (1 hour). What problem does the company solve? Who uses it? Why do they care?
- One-on-ones with 3-4 cross-functional collaborators (15-20 min each). Goal: understand their role, how you'll work with them, one question for each.
- Documentation deep dive. Read about domain, past architecture decisions, common gotchas.
- Task: Pick up one small well-scoped task. Goal: merge something. Not to be perfect.
- Buddy check-in (10 min). How's it going? What confused you today?

**Day 4-5 (Momentum)**:
- Deep dive into one critical system (2 hours). Understand how it works.
- Code review another person's PR. Ask questions. See what good looks like.
- Finish and ship first small task. Experience the full cycle (write, review, test, deploy).
- 1-on-1 with manager (30 min). Progress on milestones? Questions? Expectations clear?
- Team meeting or planning session. Get a sense of how decisions are made.
- Social time. Happy hour, lunch with team, or casual conversation. Build relationships.

**Deliverable**: New person can write and test code independently (even if small). Has done one full cycle. Met most of the team.

### Step 4: Identify Key Contacts & Relationships
Map the web of people they need to know and why.

**By type**:

**Immediate team** (meet by day 3):
- Manager: 1-on-1 meetings, feedback, career development
- Buddy: onboarding support, safe person to ask dumb questions
- Core collaborators: people they'll work with daily

**Cross-functional** (meet by day 7):
- Product/Design: understand requirements, constraints, customer context
- Infrastructure/Ops: understand systems, deployment, monitoring
- Customer Success/Sales: understand customer needs, market, go-to-market
- Finance/Business: understand business model, metrics, constraints

**Leadership** (meet by day 14):
- Functional leader (not their manager, but head of eng/product/etc.): strategy, direction, culture
- CEO/Head of Company (brief): context on company, why it matters
- Skip-level manager (manager's manager): understand reporting chain, career path

**Optional** (meet by day 30):
- Customers (if relevant): understand real problems
- Team members outside their immediate function: broader context

For each contact, note:
- **Who**: Name, role
- **Why**: What do they do, why you'll interact
- **When**: When to meet (day, week)
- **Suggested topic**: What to talk about or ask them about

### Step 5: Create Tool/Environment Setup Checklist
Make this specific to your company. Generic checklist doesn't help.

**Development**:
- Laptop setup (OS, installed languages, tools)
- Repository access and local environment (can run tests, build project)
- Code editor setup (VSCode, IDE, etc.)
- GitHub/GitLab access and permissions
- Test environment access (ability to deploy to staging)

**Communication**:
- Slack: installed, added to channels
- Email: configured, added to mailing lists
- Calendar: access to team calendar, sync personal calendar
- Video tools: Zoom, Google Meet, etc. tested

**Systems & Data**:
- Database access (can query, understand schema)
- Analytics/monitoring tools (Mixpanel, DataDog, etc.)
- Documentation wiki or Google Drive
- Customer data systems (CRM, support tools, etc.)
- Financial systems (expense management, timesheets, etc.)

**Administrative**:
- Benefits enrollment
- Tax documents (W-4, I-9, etc.)
- Direct deposit
- Insurance (health, dental, vision)
- 401k, equity agreements
- Company policies reviewed

Create a simple checkbox: can they do each thing unassisted? If not, help them.

### Step 6: Document Team Norms & Culture
Put the unwritten rules in writing. This removes assumptions.

**How decisions are made**:
- Do you discuss in meetings or async in docs?
- Who gets a say vs. who decides?
- How much data do you need before deciding?
- Can people disagree openly, or is it hierarchical?

**Communication style**:
- Synchronous (calls, meetings) or asynchronous (Slack, docs)?
- Direct feedback or indirect?
- How urgent is email vs. Slack vs. urgent Slack?
- Response time expectations (same-day, within 24h, whenever)?

**Meeting culture**:
- How many meetings per week? (Be honest.)
- Are they all optional, or expected to attend?
- Can you turn camera off?
- Is it okay to multitask?

**Work rhythm**:
- Core hours vs. flexible schedule?
- Expected to be "always on" or can you disconnect?
- How much time off do people take?
- Any team traditions (Friday lunches, retros, etc.)?

**What's valued**:
- Shipping fast or shipping quality?
- Speaking up or harmony?
- Specialization or versatility?
- Individual contribution or collaboration?

**What's frowned upon**:
- Passive-aggressive Slack?
- Shipping without testing?
- Missing standup?
- Going around people instead of direct communication?

This is not a policy document. It's "here's how we actually work."

### Step 7: Create a Reading/Watching List
Give them resources in order of importance.

**Essential** (read first 3 days):
- Company mission, vision, values (and how they manifest)
- Architecture overview (2-3 pages max)
- Your role responsibilities and success criteria
- Glossary of common terms, acronyms
- How to get help (who, when, how to ask)

**Important** (read by day 14):
- Key products/features overview
- Codebase tour (architecture, folder structure, naming conventions)
- Deployment process
- How-to guides for common tasks (deploying, running tests, debugging)
- Customer flows and user journeys

**Good to know** (read by day 30):
- Incident response process
- Code review standards
- Security policies and practices
- Performance benchmarks and optimization guidelines
- Past postmortems or decision documents

**Light reading** (anytime):
- Blog posts about why you built things this way
- Customer case studies
- Founding story or company history
- Team bios and backgrounds

Link to everything. Make it easy to search.

### Step 8: Schedule Regular Feedback Checkpoints
Don't wait until day 90 to discover things aren't working.

**Weekly** (manager, 30 min):
- How's it going? What's confusing? What are you learning?
- Feedback: one thing you did well, one thing to work on
- Clarify expectations on current task
- Remove any blockers

**Bi-weekly** (buddy, 15 min):
- Questions that come up during work
- Social check-in
- "How are people treating you?" pulse check

**Day 30** (manager, 45 min):
- Check in on day 30 milestones. Where are we?
- Feedback: bigger picture on trajectory, fit
- Clarify day 60 milestones
- Any concerns (theirs or yours)?

**Day 60** (manager, 45 min):
- Check in on day 60 milestones
- Feedback: are they independent yet? Fitting in?
- What should they focus on for day 90?
- Bigger career conversation: is this what they expected?

**Day 90** (manager, 60 min):
- Check in on day 90 milestones
- Overall assessment: are they successful in the role?
- Feedback: strengths, areas to develop
- 6-month goals and expectations
- Compensation/title review (if applicable)

Make these calendar reminders. Don't skip them because you're busy.

### Step 9: Prepare for Common Newbie Anxieties
Anticipate concerns and address them proactively.

**"I'm afraid to ask dumb questions"**
- Solution: Buddy system. It's the buddy's job to answer everything.
- Frame: "Everyone asks the same questions in the first month. That's normal."

**"I don't know if I'm doing well"**
- Solution: Weekly feedback checkpoints. They'll know where they stand.
- Frame: "I'll let you know directly if you're on track or not."

**"I'm not productive yet"**
- Solution: Set expectations. Day 30 is still onboarding, not full speed.
- Frame: "The goal in the first month is learning, not shipping. That comes in months 2-3."

**"Everyone else seems so competent and fast"**
- Solution: Remind them of how long it took the others to learn.
- Frame: "They also took 60 days to get up to speed. You're on track."

**"The culture seems cliquey"**
- Solution: Make sure they meet people outside their immediate team.
- Frame: "Here's who you should grab coffee with."

**"What if I don't like it here?"**
- Solution: Open conversation. Better to know now than month 4.
- Frame: "Let's talk honestly about fit. What's different from what you expected?"

### Step 10: Build the Actual Onboarding Document
Pull it all together into a single, scannable document.

**Structure**:
1. Welcome message (from manager, personal + context)
2. First week overview (what to expect, who you'll meet, what you'll accomplish)
3. First week checklist (day-by-day, with times and who to work with)
4. 30-60-90 day milestones (what success looks like at each gate)
5. Key people and relationships (who to meet, why, suggested topics)
6. Tool and environment setup (checklist with owners for help)
7. Team norms and culture (how we actually work)
8. Reading list (in priority order, with links)
9. FAQs (questions they'll ask, answered in advance)
10. Manager contact (how to reach you, open door policy)

Make it visual (use headers, bold key terms, add names with roles).
Keep it under 10 pages if possible. Link to detailed docs instead of embedding everything.

## Quality Gates

1. **Specific, not generic**: Does this guide reflect YOUR team, culture, and role, or could it work for any company?
2. **Milestones are measurable**: Could someone look at these at day 30 and say "yes, we hit these" or "no, we didn't"?
3. **First week is structured**: Is every day mapped out with specific activities and owners?
4. **Buddy is identified**: Is someone explicitly responsible for answering newbie questions?
5. **Key people are named**: Not "meet the product team," but "meet Sarah (Head of Product) to understand how features get prioritized."
6. **Tool setup is complete**: Can they actually set up everything on day 1, or will they be blocked?
7. **Culture is documented**: Do you explain the unwritten rules so they don't have to figure them out?
8. **Feedback is regular**: Are there checkpoints at 30/60/90, not just a surprise review?

## Examples

### Good: First Week Day 1 (Specific)
"**Day 1: Arrival & Welcome**
- 9:00 AM: Manager 1-on-1 (30 min). Venue: [Manager's office/Zoom link]. Topics: welcome, here's your laptop, here's your buddy [Buddy name], questions?
- 9:30 AM: IT setup with [IT person] (1 hour). Venue: [Office or remote]. Setup laptop, email, Slack, GitHub access. You should be able to push a branch to GitHub by 10:30 AM.
- 10:30 AM: Buddy coffee (20 min). Venue: [Kitchen/Slack]. Your buddy is [Buddy name], engineer on Team X. They'll answer setup questions and show you the ropes. Grab coffee together.
- 11:00 AM: Architecture overview from [Senior Engineer] (1.5 hours). Venue: [Meeting room]. You'll learn: how our systems fit together, what tech stack we use, where to find documentation. Bring questions.
- 12:30 PM: Lunch with team (1 hour). Venue: [Location]. Meet your team. Casual. Someone will guide you.
- 1:30 PM: Development environment setup on your own (2 hours). Venue: Your desk. Follow [this guide]. The goal: clone our main repo, run tests, see them pass. Ask [buddy name] if stuck.
- 3:30 PM: End-of-day check-in with manager (15 min). Quick call. How are you feeling? What do you need?
**By end of day, you should have**: Laptop set up, email working, Slack configured, can clone repo and run tests, met your team, know who your buddy is."

### Good: 30-Day Milestones (Specific & Measurable)
"**Day 30 Milestones** (success = you're functional, you know who everyone is)
- Can write and run code independently. You've shipped 2-3 small features or bug fixes with code review. Quality is acceptable.
- Shadowed 4 critical processes: customer onboarding flow, code deployment, incident response, weekly planning meeting.
- 1-on-1 with each of 5 key people: [PM name], [Design lead], [Infra engineer], [CS lead], [Sales rep]. You understand their role and how you'll work with them.
- Read all 'Essential' documents. You know our mission, architecture basics, glossary. Asking informed questions.
- Attended team standup, planning, retro. You know how your team works and what they're focused on.
- Identified one thing that's confusing or broken (even small). You asked about it without feeling like you should know it. Shows healthy curiosity."

### Good: Team Norms (Specific & Actionable)
"**How We Work**:
- Decisions: Small decisions happen in Slack (proposal + 24h for feedback). Big decisions (roadmap, hires, strategy) happen in documented proposals (5 pages max) + sync meeting. You propose, we discuss, [manager name] decides.
- Communication: Async-first (Slack/docs). Meetings are for discussion, not information transfer. No one is expected to read Slack while coding. Core hours for meetings are 10 AM–4 PM.
- Feedback: Direct and frequent. If something's not working, we say it quickly. It's not personal; it's how we improve. You'll get feedback in 1-on-1s and ad-hoc.
- Shipping: We ship when features are 80% done, not 100%. We iterate based on user feedback. Done is better than perfect.
- What's valued: Ownership (you own your work end-to-end). Transparency (you share what's working and what's not). Asking for help (asking questions is strength, not weakness).
- What's frowned upon: Shipping without testing. Ghosting people without communication. Passive-aggressive Slack. Going around people instead of direct conversation."

### Bad: Generic, Unmeasurable
"**Day 30**: Learn the codebase. Meet people. Get comfortable. Understand our product. Start contributing."

No specifics. "Learn the codebase" is vague. "Meet people" doesn't say who. "Start contributing" doesn't say what. "Get comfortable" is not measurable.

### Bad: Missing Structure
"We'll figure out onboarding as you go. You'll probably shadow someone. Let me know if you need anything."

No plan. No accountability. New person is anxious. No structure = slow ramp.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Generic checklist instead of personalized guide**: "Complete HR paperwork, set up computer, read docs" tells them nothing about YOUR role, team, or culture.

2. **No clear owner for tasks**: "Someone will show you around" = no one does. "Your buddy is Sarah. She'll show you around Wednesday at 2 PM" = it happens.

3. **Too many meetings too early**: Day 1: 8 meetings. New person is overwhelmed and retains nothing. Spread key meetings over first 3 weeks.

4. **Assuming they know what they don't**: "Set up your environment" works for someone who's done it at 3 companies. New grad needs step-by-step guide.

5. **No real connection with their manager**: Day 1 welcome is 5 minutes. Real onboarding requires 30 min/week with manager for the first month. If you don't have time, you're not ready to hire.

6. **Documentation is generic**: "Read our architecture docs." Which docs? In what order? Why does each one matter? Prioritize and explain why.

7. **Milestones are aspirational, not realistic**: "Day 30: Led a major project." Most people can't do that. Better: "Day 30: Shipped 3 small features, understand the codebase, met key stakeholders."

## Anti-Patterns

1. **"Sink or swim" onboarding**: No structure. Day 1 they sit at a desk with no laptop set up. By week 2, they're reading documentation alone. This is how you lose good hires.

2. **Bottleneck manager**: Onboarding guide says "ask your manager." But manager is in 20 meetings a day and answers in 48 hours. Get a buddy to reduce dependency.

3. **Onboarding guide that no one follows**: Beautiful 20-page document. No one sends it. No one checks milestones. Pointless. Make it easy to follow and hold people accountable.

4. **Assuming cultural osmosis**: "You'll pick up how we work." Some people will. Others will spend 6 months confused. Write it down.

5. **Delaying the hard conversation**: "You're struggling a bit" at day 60 when they could have heard weekly feedback and adjusted. Weekly feedback + early course correction = better outcomes.
