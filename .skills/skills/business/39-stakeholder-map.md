# Stakeholder Map Generator

## Purpose
A stakeholder map identifies everyone affected by or influencing a decision/project, assesses their power (ability to block or accelerate) and interest (whether they care), and develops tailored engagement strategies. This skill generates stakeholder inventories, power/interest grids, and communication plans. Output enables: proactive alignment, reduced surprises, smoother execution, and stakeholder satisfaction.

## When to Use
- Launching a major project or initiative (cross-functional)
- Making strategic decisions affecting multiple teams
- M&A, fundraising, or significant market moves
- Change management (new process, system, or structure)
- Handling resistance or stakeholder conflict
- **Do NOT use when**: Decision is unilateral (your choice only); stakeholders are few and aligned; you're in crisis (alignment takes time you don't have).

## Instructions

### Step 1: Identify All Stakeholders
Stakeholders are anyone affected by or influencing the decision. Include:
- **Decision-makers** (have authority)
- **Influencers** (have sway without authority)
- **Executors** (implement the decision)
- **Impactees** (affected by outcome, may have no input)
- **Gatekeepers** (control access/resources needed)

Don't miss quiet stakeholders (often most important).

**Brainstorm by Role**:
- Executive team: CEO, CFO, CTO, COO
- Department heads: VP Sales, VP Product, VP Ops
- Team leads: Engineering lead, Design lead, Finance lead
- Individual contributors: Key engineers, sales reps
- Customers: Advisory board, key accounts
- Partners: Vendors, investors, board members
- External: Regulators, media, industry analysts

**Example: Pricing Strategy Stakeholder List**:
- CEO (decision authority)
- CFO (financial impact)
- VP Sales (customer reaction)
- VP Product (feature/value impact)
- Customer Advisory Board (willing to pay {{$$}}?)
- Key Customers ({{#}} large accounts, 60% of revenue)
- Board Members (shareholder perspective)
- Finance Team (modeling impact)

### Step 2: Assess Power (Influence) & Interest
For each stakeholder, estimate:

**Power (Ability to block or accelerate)**:
- High: Can veto decision, control resources needed, has executive authority
- Medium: Has input and influence, but decision doesn't require their approval
- Low: Affected but no veto power or resource control

**Interest (How much they care)**:
- High: Directly impacted, motivated to engage, will voice opinions
- Medium: Somewhat affected, may or may not engage
- Low: Minimally affected, unlikely to engage

**Example Assessments**:
- CEO: Power=High (final decision), Interest=High (impacts revenue & profitability)
- VP Sales: Power=Medium (can influence), Interest=High (sales impact)
- Customer (non-strategic): Power=Low, Interest=Medium (product affects them, but individual account is replaceable)
- Key Customer (top 20% revenue): Power=High (can defect), Interest=High (pricing affects them significantly)

### Step 3: Plot Power/Interest Grid
Create a 2×2 matrix:

```
             High Interest  |  Low Interest
High Power   Quadrant 1     |  Quadrant 2
             (Manage        |  (Keep
              Closely)       |   Satisfied)
────────────────────────────┼───────────────
Low Power    Quadrant 3     |  Quadrant 4
             (Keep          |  (Monitor)
              Informed)      |
```

**Quadrant 1 (High Power, High Interest): Manage Closely**
- CEO, CFO, major customers
- Strategy: Involve early, solicit input, keep updated, build consensus

**Quadrant 2 (High Power, Low Interest): Keep Satisfied**
- Board members who aren't engaged in day-to-day but could block decision
- Strategy: Inform, don't demand engagement, but keep satisfied with simple summary

**Quadrant 3 (Low Power, High Interest): Keep Informed**
- Team members affected, customer advisory board
- Strategy: Share information, listen to concerns, but don't make them central to decision-making

**Quadrant 4 (Low Power, Low Interest): Monitor**
- Peripheral stakeholders
- Strategy: Minimal engagement, basic updates only

### Step 4: Identify Concerns & Interests
What does each stakeholder care about? What could go wrong from their perspective?

**CEO**: Revenue, profit margin, competitive positioning, risk
**VP Sales**: Customer acceptance, ease of selling, competitive response
**CFO**: Unit economics, margin, cash impact
**Customer**: Value for money, no disruption, continued quality
**Key Customer**: Negotiate better price, special terms

Understanding concerns informs engagement strategy.

### Step 5: Develop Engagement Strategy per Quadrant
Different stakeholders need different approaches.

**Quadrant 1 (Manage Closely)**:
- **Timing**: Involve early (weeks before decision)
- **Format**: Meetings, working sessions, data review
- **Information**: Full data, trade-offs, reasoning
- **Expectation**: Consensus or at least buy-in

**Quadrant 2 (Keep Satisfied)**:
- **Timing**: Inform before public announcement
- **Format**: Summary memo, brief call
- **Information**: High-level rationale, no debate
- **Expectation**: No objection (they already delegated to others)

**Quadrant 3 (Keep Informed)**:
- **Timing**: Announce after decision made
- **Format**: All-hands, team meeting, email
- **Information**: How decision affects them, next steps
- **Expectation**: Understanding, not input

**Quadrant 4 (Monitor)**:
- **Timing**: Include in broadcast communications
- **Format**: Announcement email
- **Information**: Basic facts, no detail
- **Expectation**: Awareness

### Step 6: Plan Communication & Rollout Timeline
Map stakeholder engagement to decision timeline.

**Timeline Example (Pricing Change Announcement)**:
- **Week 1**: Private meeting with Quadrant 1 (CEO, CFO, VP Sales, top 3 customers)
  - Discuss strategy, get buy-in, address concerns
- **Week 2**: Informational call with Quadrant 2 (Board members)
  - Brief them, answer questions, lock support
- **Week 3**: All-hands announcement to Quadrant 3 (team)
  - Explain rationale, address fears, commit to support
- **Week 4**: Public announcement and rollout
  - Pricing goes live

Sequence matters. If Quadrant 1 isn't aligned, week 1 fails and everything else fails.

### Step 7: Identify & Address Concerns Proactively
What objections will each quadrant raise? Address them upfront.

**Example (Pricing Change)**:
- **VP Sales** will worry: "Customers will churn."
  - Address: Show win/loss data ({{X%}} accept new pricing, {{Y%}} churn). Show migration path. Offer sales support/messaging.
- **Customer (strategic)** will worry: "We're being price-gouged; should we switch vendors?"
  - Address: Transparent value story (cost savings they get), special pricing for early adopters, contract extension with lock.
- **Team** will worry: "We're raising prices for greed."
  - Address: Show unit economics (we're losing money at current price), growth plan enabled by new pricing.

### Step 8: Create Communication Plan
Who communicates what, to whom, when, and how?

**Communication Matrix**:
| Stakeholder | Message | Format | Messenger | Timing | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CEO | Full strategy & data | Meeting | CFO | Week 1 | Once |
| VP Sales | Customer talking points | Email + call | CEO | Week 2 | Once + weekly syncs |
| Customers | New pricing + value | Email + call | VP Sales or Account Manager | Week 4 | Once (followed up) |
| Team | Rationale + support | All-hands meeting | CEO | Week 3 | Once (FAQs after) |

### Step 9: Monitor & Adjust
As you execute, monitor stakeholder sentiment and adjust strategy.

**Monitoring**:
- After each communication: Did they understand? Any concerns?
- Before next phase: Are they aligned? Any red flags?
- Post-decision: Are they executing? Any resistance?

If issues arise, escalate and course-correct.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Missing Key Stakeholders**: You engage {{visible}} stakeholders but miss {{quiet]] one with veto power (IT, legal, finance). They object late, derail decision. Better: Brainstorm by function and role; include "soft power" people.

2. **Same Engagement for All**: Email announcement to Quadrant 1 (same as Quadrant 4). CEO feels disrespected; doesn't build consensus. Better: Tailor engagement. Quadrant 1 gets multiple touches, Quadrant 4 gets one email.

3. **Objections Not Addressed Upfront**: You know VP Sales will worry about commission. But don't address until they raise it Week 3 (late). Better: Identify concern upfront, address in Week 1 meeting with data. No surprises.

4. **No Sequencing**: Announce to board before CEO aligns. Board asks CEO's opinion; CEO hasn't decided. Chaos. Better: Quadrant 1 → Quadrant 2 → Quadrant 3 → Quadrant 4 (top-down cascade).

5. **Communication From Wrong Messenger**: CFO announces sales strategy to customers. Lacks credibility. Better: {{CEO or VP Sales}} announces sales-related decisions.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Stakeholder Engagement Theater**: You involve stakeholders but don't actually listen. Decision was made before engagement. Stakeholders feel used. Better: Listen genuinely. Be willing to adjust decision if feedback is compelling.

2. **Snowballing Surprises**: Quadrant 1 told Week 1, but news leaks. Quadrant 3 hears through grapevine before all-hands. Loss of control. Better: Explicit embargo on communication until {{date}}. Brief stakeholders on embargo.

3. **No Disagreement Management**: Key Customer disagrees on pricing. You say "We're doing it anyway." They churn. Better: Option 1: Adjust decision if {{customer critical]]. Option 2: Offer {{mitigation (contract lock, discount)}} to reduce churn. Option 3: Accept churn loss as trade-off, but don't surprise yourself.

4. **Stakeholder Engagement Without Accountability**: You make a plan to engage {{VP Sales}} Week 1. Week 1 arrives, nobody scheduled meeting. Engagement falls apart. Better: Assign owner. "{{CEO]] will meet {{VP Sales]] on {{Monday 10am]]." Lock calendar. Execute.

5. **No Post-Decision Monitoring**: You announce, move on. But {{key customer]] is quietly looking at {{alternatives]]. You find out only after they churn. Better: Post-announcement, monitor {{sentiment]]. Weekly check-in calls with Quadrant 1. Early warning.
