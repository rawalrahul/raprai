# Curriculum Designer

## Purpose
This skill generates complete curriculum frameworks that align standards to learning outcomes, sequence content logically, embed assessments throughout (not just at the end), plan differentiation for diverse learners, and map resources efficiently. A well-designed curriculum is coherent (each piece connects), rigorous (standards-aligned, cognitively challenging), equitable (accessible to all learners), and assessable (clear evidence of learning). This skill guides design thinking for entire courses, units, or interdisciplinary programs.

## When to Use
- Designing a new course or complete curriculum overhaul
- Creating a semester or year-long sequence of units
- Building interdisciplinary or project-based curricula
- Aligning curriculum to new standards
- Designing curricula that serve diverse learners (ELL, gifted, students with IEPs)
- **Do NOT use when**: Planning individual lessons (use lesson-planner), creating assessments (use quiz-generator, rubric-builder), or teaching a single skill (use tutorial-writer)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Conduct Needs Analysis and Define Context
Identify the purpose of the curriculum: Who are the learners? (grade level, prior knowledge, demographics, learning needs) What is the context? (content standards required, time available, resources available, community/school context) What are learners' needs? (skill gaps, interests, career/college readiness) What are constraints? (time, materials, technology, teacher expertise).

Example: "Grade 10 Biology Course. Students: ages 15-16, mixed reading levels (several ELL students, 1 student on IEP for math), interested in health/fitness and environmental issues. Time: 1 semester (18 weeks, 1 hour daily). Standards: State Biology Standards requiring understanding of cellular biology, genetics, evolution, ecology. Resources: limited (no lab equipment beyond basic); strong library access. Constraint: Co-taught with special education teacher 3 periods per week."

This context shapes everything downstream—content selection, pacing, differentiation, assessment.

### Step 2: Identify and Align Standards and Learning Outcomes
List all relevant standards (state standards, Common Core if applicable, national standards like NGSS). For each standard, translate it into a student-friendly learning outcome. Outcomes should be specific, measurable, and at multiple Bloom's levels (not all Recall; include Analyze/Evaluate).

Example standards → outcomes:

**Standard (State Biology 10.1)**: "Students will understand cell structure and function"
**Outcome (Remember)**: Students will identify and define cell organelles
**Outcome (Understand)**: Students will explain how organelles work together to perform cell functions
**Outcome (Apply)**: Students will predict how changes to organelles (e.g., damaged mitochondria) affect cell function
**Outcome (Analyze)**: Students will compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, identifying structural differences that explain functional differences

All standards should be represented; prioritize standards that appear in high-stakes assessments (AP, SAT, state exams). Create a standards alignment document showing which outcomes map to which standards.

### Step 3: Design the Curriculum Map (Scope and Sequence)
Create a visual overview showing: (a) Major units or themes, (b) Sequence (order they're taught), (c) Duration of each, (d) How they connect to each other, (e) Pacing milestones.

Example 18-week Biology curriculum:
| Week | Unit | Focus | Duration | Connection to Previous Unit |
|------|------|-------|----------|----------------------------|
| 1-3 | Cell Structure & Function | Organelles, prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic, cell membrane | 3 weeks | Foundation for all life |
| 4-5 | Photosynthesis & Cellular Respiration | Energy capture and use; how organisms power themselves | 2 weeks | Builds on cell function; connects to organism energy needs |
| 6-8 | Genetics & Heredity | Mendelian genetics, DNA, protein synthesis, mutation | 3 weeks | Explains how traits pass; foundation for evolution |
| 9-11 | Evolution | Natural selection, evidence, speciation, adaptation | 3 weeks | Explains diversity of life; applies to ecology |
| 12-14 | Ecology | Population, community, ecosystem, biomes | 3 weeks | Applies evolution and genetics to real populations |
| 15-16 | Integrated Project | Students design solution to environmental problem using all prior knowledge | 2 weeks | Culminating experience integrating all units |
| 17-18 | Review & Assessment | Final exam; student reflections | 2 weeks | Synthesis |

Sequencing rationale: Start with cell (foundational), move to organism level (how cells function in organisms), then population level (how organisms interact). Each unit assumes prior knowledge; later units apply earlier learning.

### Step 4: Create Unit Plans (4-7 units; each with detailed structure)
For each major unit, specify: (a) Unit title and essential question, (b) Learning outcomes (mapped to standards, differentiated by Bloom's level), (c) Key concepts and vocabulary, (d) Instructional sequence (how you'll teach it), (e) Formative assessments (checks throughout), (f) Summative assessment (final task), (g) Differentiation strategies for the unit, (h) Resources needed.

Example Unit 1: Cell Structure and Function

**Essential Question**: "How do organelles enable cells to survive and function?"

**Learning Outcomes**:
- Remember: Identify organelles and describe their location within the cell
- Understand: Explain how organelles perform specific functions needed for life
- Apply: Predict how changes to organelles affect cell survival
- Analyze: Compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells and explain why eukaryotic cells are more complex

**Key Concepts**: Organelle structure, prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic, cell membrane, homeostasis, cell division

**Vocabulary**: Nucleus, mitochondrion, chloroplast, ribosome, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosome, cell membrane, prokaryote, eukaryote, etc.

**Instructional Sequence**:
- Days 1-2: Hook (cell observation under microscope); mini-lecture on cell types
- Days 3-5: Guided exploration of organelles (labels, functions, visual models)
- Days 6-8: Differentiated activities: Below-level (label and match), At-level (explain function), Advanced (compare/predict effects)
- Day 9: Formative assessment (organelle function quiz with analysis questions)
- Days 10-12: Prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic comparison (Venn diagram, analytical discussion)

**Formative Assessments**:
- Observation during microscope work (can students identify structures?)
- Quick quiz on organelle names/locations (remember level)
- Think-pair-share: "Why might a cell need a nucleus?" (understand level)
- Prediction task: "A cell's mitochondria stop working. What happens?" (apply level)

**Summative Assessment**:
Performance task—Students design a model eukaryotic cell (physical model, poster, or digital) and explain (written or oral) how each organelle contributes to the cell's survival. Rubric measures understanding of organelle function and ability to communicate.

**Differentiation**:
- Below-level: Provide organelle function cards and cell diagram; students match. Provide sentence frames for written explanation.
- At-level: Standard task; students conduct own research on organelle functions.
- Advanced: Design a cell from an extreme environment (arctic, desert, deep-sea) and explain how organelles are adapted for that environment.

**Resources**:
- Microscopes and prepared slides
- Cell model kits (3-4 available)
- Video: "Tour of the Cell" (TED-Ed, 5 min)
- Interactive: Khan Academy cell structure
- Handouts: Organelle function cards, comparison templates
- Library: Biology textbooks, access to online databases

### Step 5: Define Assessment Strategy (Diagnostic, Formative, Summative)
Create an assessment blueprint showing: (a) What you assess (which outcomes), (b) When (diagnostic at start, formative throughout, summative at end), (c) How (quiz, project, presentation, exam), (d) Weight toward grade.

Example assessment blueprint for 18-week course:
| Outcome/Standard | Diagnostic (Pre-Unit) | Formative (During Unit) | Summative (End of Unit) | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cell Structure & Function | Pre-quiz (organelle identification) | 3 quick checks + observation rubric | Cell design project + explanation | 15% of course grade |
| Photosynthesis & Respiration | Pre-quiz (energy concept) | Lab investigation + data analysis | Problem-solving task (design energy system) | 12% |
| Genetics | Pre-quiz (inheritance concepts) | 2 practice problem sets + peer review | Genetics problem set + Punnett square analysis | 15% |
| Evolution | Pre-quiz (adaptation concepts) | Case study analysis + discussion | Evolution argument essay | 18% |
| Ecology | Pre-quiz (ecosystem concepts) | Field data collection + analysis | Ecosystem simulation + recommendations | 15% |
| Integration/Synthesis | — | Formative reflection throughout | Integrated capstone project | 25% |

Diagnostic assessments identify prior knowledge gaps; formative assessments guide instruction; summative assessments measure mastery. Ensure: (1) Each outcome is assessed multiple times (never assess once and move on), (2) Assessments at appropriate Bloom's level (high-order outcomes shouldn't be assessed only with recall questions), (3) Clear grading scale and rubrics for each assessment.

### Step 6: Plan Differentiation and Inclusive Design
Differentiation is not one lesson for everyone with some homework extensions. It's intentional, planned adjustments so all learners can access rigorous content at appropriate complexity. Design for: (a) Readiness (prior knowledge, reading level, skill level), (b) Interest (student interests shape examples and projects), (c) Learning profile (visual, kinesthetic, linguistic preferences).

Specific strategies:

**For Advanced Learners**: Provide complexity through depth (deeper analysis, transfer to new contexts) or breadth (explore topics beyond core curriculum). Example: While classmates complete organelle function task, advanced students research organelle structure at the molecular level and create detailed models.

**For Struggling Learners**: Provide scaffolding (structure, partially worked examples, sentence frames, checklists), explicit teaching of prerequisite skills, alternative representation (visual if reading is difficult, kinesthetic if abstract is hard), frequent formative assessment to catch confusion early.

**For ELL Students**: Pre-teach key vocabulary with visuals/translations, provide graphic organizers, allow use of native language for processing (think in home language, then English), pair with English-proficient peer, provide audio versions of text.

**For Students with IEPs**: Follow IEP requirements precisely (accommodations, modified objectives if applicable, progress monitoring). Ensure accommodations are built into curriculum design, not added as afterthoughts.

Example differentiation plan for Cell Structure unit:
- **Vocabulary Support** (all students): Key terms introduced with visuals, definitions in simple language, interactive flashcards
- **Organelle Function** (below-level): Matching task with function cards; sentence frame for explanation ("The _____ makes _____ so the cell can _____")
- **Organelle Comparison** (at-level): Venn diagram comparing organelle structures; written explanation of why differences matter
- **Prokaryotic-Eukaryotic Transfer** (advanced): Analyze why prokaryotes lack nucleus; predict how this limits their size and complexity
- **Kinesthetic Access** (all interested students): Build models with craft materials; act out organelle "workers" performing functions
- **Audio Access** (ELL, visual processing difficulty): Video explanations with captions; audio glossary
- **IEP Access** (specific student): Reduced reading load; emphasis on visual and kinesthetic; frequent breaks; extended time on assessments

### Step 7: Map Resources and Logistics
Identify: (a) Instructional materials needed (textbooks, videos, online platforms, lab materials), (b) Technology required (software, internet bandwidth, device access), (c) Facilities (lab space, field trip locations), (d) Teacher resources (professional development needed, preparation time), (e) Budget considerations.

Example resource map for Biology:
| Unit | Materials Needed | Technology | Facilities | Cost | Timeline to Prepare |
|------|------------------|-----------|-----------|------|-------------------|
| Cell Structure | Microscopes (6), slides (prepared), model kits (4), handouts | Khan Academy videos, interactive simulations (free), document camera | Lab room, microscope storage | $200 (replacement slides) | Summer—purchase slides, test microscopes |
| Photosynthesis | Plants (common houseplant), light source, measuring tools, containers, soil | Simulation software (PhET free), video, data spreadsheet | Classroom (window or grow lights) | $75 (grow lights, plant supplies) | June—acquire lights, test setup |
| Genetics | Punnett square templates, dice, cards, fruit fly cultures (if doing lab) | Online Punnett square tool (free), virtual lab alternative (Carolina Biological, cost if used) | Classroom or lab (if live organism lab) | $0-300 depending on lab choices | July—order fruit fly cultures if doing live lab |

Realistic resource inventory: What do you have? What must you purchase or borrow? What free alternatives exist? Build curriculum around available resources; note where digital alternatives work if physical materials unavailable.

### Step 8: Create Pacing Guide (Realistic Timeline)
Design a week-by-week pacing guide showing: (a) Week number, (b) Unit/topic, (c) Key activities/lessons, (d) Formative assessments, (e) Holidays/breaks that disrupt pacing.

Example pacing (18-week semester):
| Week | Unit | Focus | Activities | Formative Assessment |
|------|------|-------|-----------|---------------------|
| 1 | Cell Structure | Introduction to cells | Hook: cell observation under microscope; mini-lecture | Observation checklist |
| 2 | Cell Structure | Organelles | Guided exploration; organelle function cards | Quick quiz (names/locations) |
| 3 | Cell Structure | Comparison | Prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic; lab activity | Venn diagram analysis |
| 4 | Photosynthesis | Light reactions | Mini-lecture; modeling activity | Worksheet with analysis questions |
| 5 | Photosynthesis | Dark reactions & cellular respiration | Comparison; problem-solving | Practice problems |
| **6** | **Review/Exams** | **Midterm** | **Study guide review; formative exam** | **Midterm exam** |
| 7-8 | Genetics | Mendelian genetics | Punnett square practice; problem sets | Practice problem assessment |
| 9 | Genetics | DNA & protein synthesis | Lab or simulation | Data analysis worksheet |
| 10 | Evolution | Natural selection | Case study; reading; discussion | Comprehension check |
| 11-12 | Evolution | Speciation, evidence | Debate preparation; research | Argument essay draft |
| 13-14 | Ecology | Ecosystem interactions | Field data collection; simulations | Data analysis |
| 15-16 | **Capstone Project** | **Integrated project** | **Design environmental solution using all units** | **Project milestone checks** |
| **17** | **Final Assessment** | **Final exam & student reflection** | **Comprehensive review** | **Final exam** |
| **18** | **Buffer** | **Grade wrap-up** | **Reteaching based on gaps** | **Re-assessment if needed** |

Include realistic: (a) Holiday breaks (winter, spring breaks), (b) Time for reteaching (if formative assessments show gaps), (c) Time for review before major exams, (d) Buffer time for pacing variations (some units take longer).

### Step 9: Design Interdisciplinary Connections (if applicable)
Identify connections to other subjects or real-world contexts. Example: Cell Structure connects to chemistry (molecular bonding), physics (osmosis), health (disease), technology (microscopy).

Example interdisciplinary connections for Biology:
- **Math**: Data analysis from ecology and genetics units; graphing; statistics
- **Language Arts**: Argument writing (evolution essay); research skills (literature search); technical writing (lab reports)
- **History/Social Studies**: Evolution and society (impact of evolutionary thinking); ecology and environmental policy; ethics of genetic engineering
- **Art**: Create cell models artistically; design infographics; visualize abstract concepts

Build these connections explicitly—don't assume students will see them. Create assignments that require integration (e.g., "Write a persuasive essay using scientific evidence and addressing counterarguments" integrates English and science).

### Step 10: Plan for Ongoing Revision and Teacher Collaboration
Curriculum is never "done." Plan: (a) How you'll collect data on what's working (student assessment results, teacher reflection, student feedback), (b) Regular review meetings with colleagues, (c) Annual revision cycle (summer curriculum work), (d) How new research/standards will be incorporated.

Include: Curriculum review calendar, collaboration norms, data collection methods (which assessments show if learning is happening?), revision process.

## Quality Gates
1. **Standards Alignment**: Every learning outcome maps explicitly to standards; backwards design evident
2. **Sequencing Logic**: Units build on each other; later learning assumes earlier knowledge; rationale for sequence is clear
3. **Assessment Coherence**: Outcomes, instruction, and assessment align; each outcome assessed multiple times at appropriate Bloom's level
4. **Differentiation Integration**: Not an afterthought; planned strategies for diverse learners; includes specific accommodations
5. **Resource Realism**: Resources identified, budgeted, realistic; alternatives offered for resource constraints
6. **Pacing Honesty**: Time allocations are realistic; includes buffer for reteaching and assessment; accounts for holidays/breaks
7. **Formative Assessment Frequency**: Formative assessments throughout (not just summative at end); data informs instruction
8. **Closure/Capstone**: Unit or culminating experience that integrates learning across curriculum; not just isolated skills
9. **Interdisciplinary Connection**: Explicit connections to other subjects or real-world contexts; not siloed
10. **Iterative Design**: Plan for how curriculum will be revised based on data; not a static document

## Examples

### Good: Grade 10 Biology Curriculum (excerpts)
**Context**: 18 weeks, mixed-ability classroom (ELL, advanced, students with IEPs), no advanced lab equipment. School emphasizes environmental sustainability.

**Unit Sequence**: Cell Structure → Cellular Respiration/Photosynthesis → Genetics → Evolution → Ecology → Capstone Project

**Capstone Project**: "Design a Solution to an Environmental Problem." Students choose real environmental issue (e.g., ocean plastic, deforestation, water pollution), research causes (apply ecosystem knowledge), propose solution (apply multiple concepts), design monitoring system (apply data analysis and ecology), present to community panel.

**Differentiation for Ecology Unit**:
- Advanced: Model different scenarios (carbon tax, conservation habitat, renewable energy) and predict ecosystem impact; present recommendations to stakeholders
- At-level: Analyze real ecosystem data; identify limiting factors; propose management strategy
- Below-level: Complete partially guided ecosystem analysis; identify organisms, food chains, energy flow with scaffolds; propose one management idea with sentence frames
- ELL: Pre-taught key vocabulary (producer, consumer, decomposer) with visuals; work with English-proficient peer; allowed to explain thinking in native language first
- IEP (math disability): Reduced data analysis complexity; focus on conceptual understanding of ecosystem interactions; modified assessment (oral explanation instead of written report)

**Resource Reality Check**: "No money for expensive lab equipment. Solutions: Use Carolina Biological virtual labs (free), conduct field observations at nearby park, use online data sets, partner with university for guest lecture."

### Bad: Biology Curriculum
**Units**: Cell, Genetics, Evolution, Ecology, Photosynthesis
- No clear sequencing or rationale
- Learning outcomes: "Understand cells," "Know about genetics" (vague)
- Assessment: Multiple-choice midterm and final (all recall level)
- No formative assessments or feedback
- No differentiation mentioned
- No resource planning
- Generic, disjointed, doesn't reflect standards alignment or student needs

## Common Mistakes

1. **Standards as Learning Outcomes**: Listing standards directly as outcomes. Standards are what students should know; outcomes are how you'll measure they know it. Instead: Translate standards into specific, measurable outcomes at multiple Bloom's levels.

2. **Isolated Units Without Sequencing**: Units exist but don't build on each other. Later units don't assume earlier learning. Instead: Design explicit connections—Unit 2 applies Unit 1 concepts; Unit 3 assumes Unit 2 mastery. Create a rationale for sequence.

3. **Assessment All at the End**: Instruction → Instruction → Summative assessment. If students fail, no data tells you where comprehension broke down. Instead: Embed formative assessment throughout. Adjust instruction based on formative data before summative assessment.

4. **Differentiation as Add-On**: "Regular curriculum + optional enrichment for advanced/extra help for struggling students." This leaves most students in one-size-fits-all pace. Instead: Design differentiation into core curriculum. All students access the same standards but at different complexity levels.

5. **Ignoring Constraints**: Curriculum assumes unlimited time, resources, teacher expertise. When implemented, it's cut/modified. Instead: Design within constraints. If no lab equipment, use simulations. If limited tech, use low-tech alternatives. Make this part of curriculum, not a later adjustment.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Textbook as Curriculum**: Following textbook chapter order instead of standards-based design. Textbooks are resources, not curricula. Instead: Start with standards and outcomes, then use textbooks selectively to support learning.

2. **Coverage Over Depth**: "We need to cover everything, so we go fast." Students memorize facts but don't deeply understand. Instead: Prioritize standards. Explicitly choose what to teach in depth (focus standards) vs. what to touch lightly (supporting standards). Depth beats coverage.

3. **Disconnected Assessments**: Assessment tasks don't match what instruction emphasized; high-stakes test doesn't align to curriculum. Instead: Work backwards from summative assessment. Instruction prepares students for the specific assessment task they'll face.

4. **Equity Erasure**: "All students take the same path at the same pace. That's fair." This ignores that students enter with different skills. Instead: Equity is meeting students where they are and providing what they need to reach the same rigorous standards. Differentiation is equity.
