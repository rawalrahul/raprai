# Decision Maker

## Purpose

Difficult decisions paralyze because we weigh options intuitively, flip-flop on what matters, and fear making the wrong choice. This skill provides three complementary frameworks—weighted criteria scoring, regret minimization, and values alignment—to structure the decision transparently, expose your true priorities, and commit to a choice with confidence. You'll exit with both a recommendation and a rationale you can defend.

## When to Use

- You're stuck between 2-5 viable options
- The stakes are meaningful (affects career, relationships, finances, time)
- You've been deliberating for more than a week
- You can't articulate why you prefer one option
- You need to explain your reasoning to others (boss, partner, board)
- You want to avoid post-decision regret and second-guessing
- **Do NOT use when**: The decision is trivial (takes <5 minutes to reverse or low cost), only one viable option exists, or the choice is purely dependent on external information you don't have yet

## Instructions

### Step 1: Define the Decision and Constraint

State the decision as a clear question: "Should I accept the job offer or stay in my current role?" or "Which cloud provider should we use for the new platform?"

List hard constraints—non-negotiables that eliminate options:
- Budget cap: $50k/year max
- Timeline: Must decide by March 15
- Geographic: Must be within 30 miles of family
- Outcome: Must support remote work

Eliminate options that violate constraints immediately. Don't score them; they're off the table.

### Step 2: Weighted Criteria Matrix (The Rational Scorecard)

List all viable options across the top. Identify 5-10 evaluation criteria (examples: cost, growth potential, team culture, commute, job security, creative freedom, work-life balance).

For each criterion:
1. **Weight it**: How important is this (1-5 scale)? Sum all weights; normalize so they total 100. Higher weight = more decision-influencing.
2. **Score each option** (1-10 scale): How well does each option perform on this criterion?
3. **Calculate weighted score**: (Score × Weight) for each option
4. **Sum**: Total weighted score per option

Example table:

```
Criterion          | Weight | Offer A | Offer B | Offer C
                   | (%)    | Score/W | Score/W | Score/W
Salary             | 20%    | 9/1.8   | 7/1.4   | 8/1.6
Growth Potential   | 25%    | 8/2.0   | 9/2.25  | 6/1.5
Team Quality       | 20%    | 7/1.4   | 8/1.6   | 9/1.8
Work-Life Balance  | 20%    | 6/1.2   | 8/1.6   | 7/1.4
Commute            | 15%    | 5/0.75  | 9/1.35  | 4/0.6
TOTAL              | 100%   | 7.15    | 8.2     | 6.5
```

The matrix makes trade-offs visible. Offer B wins, but by only 1 point. Notice that if you weight growth potential less (say, 15% instead of 25%), Offer A could win.

**Key insight**: The weighting is where your values live. If salary gets 5% but growth gets 25%, you're signaling that learning matters more than money right now. Is that true?

### Step 3: Test with Regret Minimization

Imagine yourself one year from now, having chosen each option. Ask:

**For the winning option from Step 2:**
- Will you regret not choosing the other options? How much (1-10 scale)?
- In 5 years, will you view this as a good call or a missed opportunity?
- What would have to go wrong for you to deeply regret this?

**For the runner-up options:**
- Which would you regret *not* choosing the most?
- Is your regret about missing that option bigger than your concern about the risks it carries?

If regret about not choosing option B is higher than your preference for A based on the matrix, that's a signal your weights were wrong or your criteria incomplete. Revisit step 2.

Jeff Bezos's version: "In 80 years, will you regret not taking this bet?" Regret as a compass is more reliable than trying to predict outcomes.

### Step 4: Apply the 10-10-10 Framework

Ask three time-horizon questions:

1. **How will I feel about this in 10 minutes?** (Emotional/gut reaction. Don't decide here, but notice it.)
2. **How will I feel about this in 10 months?** (Medium-term practical consequences. Will the choice have paid off? Hit complications?)
3. **How will I feel about this in 10 years?** (Life trajectory impact. Does this decision still make sense in hindsight? Did it lead somewhere good?)

Write out your genuine answer for the top option(s). If your answer at 10 months is "I'll regret it," that's a veto, even if the matrix score is high. If at 10 years you see a positive arc, that often outweighs short-term friction.

This framework interrupts the tyranny of immediate emotion (10 minutes) and unexamined assumptions about long-term payoff.

### Step 5: Alignment with Core Values

List your top 5 personal values: autonomy, financial security, creativity, family time, impact, learning, adventure, status, etc.

Score each option (1-10) on how well it aligns with each value:

```
Value              | Weight | Option A | Option B | Option C
Autonomy           | High   | 8        | 5        | 7
Financial Security | High   | 6        | 8        | 7
Learning           | High   | 9        | 8        | 5
Family Time        | High   | 5        | 9        | 7
Impact             | Med    | 7        | 6        | 8
```

This often reveals a clear winner (or clarifies what you value). If you've been conflicted, it's often because the options align with different values you hold equally. The solution: acknowledge the trade-off, don't pretend there isn't one.

### Step 6: Decision Rule and Threshold

Based on all four frameworks, establish your decision rule:

- **Unanimous win**: Matrix winner matches regret minimization, 10-10-10, and values alignment. Decide immediately.
- **Split decision**: Frameworks disagree (matrix picks A, gut says B). Revisit weighting or criteria; you may have missed something. Or acknowledge it's a genuine trade-off.
- **Threshold rule**: "If the matrix score gap is >2 points AND regret is low AND 10-year view is positive, I choose the winner. Otherwise, I delay and gather more info."

### Step 7: Document and Commit

Write a 1-page decision memo:

**Decision**: [Clear recommendation]

**Key Criteria and Weights**: [The top 3 factors driving the choice]

**Matrix Summary**: [Final scores or rough table]

**Regret Test**: [Honestly, will you regret not picking the runner-up?]

**10-10-10 Reflection**: [Key insights from each timeframe]

**Values Alignment**: [How this choice honors your priorities]

**Contingency**: [If X happens, I'll revisit this decision]

Share this memo with a trusted advisor. Their reaction often surfaces doubts or validates your reasoning.

## Quality Gates (5+)

1. **Constraints Applied**: Did you eliminate options that violate hard constraints? (If not, you're not taking constraints seriously.)
2. **Weights Defensible**: Can you explain why you weighted autonomy at 25% and salary at 20%? Do the weights reflect your actual values?
3. **Regret Realistic**: Did you honestly assess regret, or did you rationalize? (If regret score is <3 for all options, you may not be being honest.)
4. **10-Year View Positive**: Can you genuinely see yourself satisfied with this choice a decade out? If not, what's the real concern?
5. **No Obvious Omissions**: Did you miss a critical criterion or option? Spend 2 minutes brainstorming "what would change my mind?"
6. **Accountability Built In**: Did you specify a contingency trigger to revisit? (E.g., "If X company makes an offer by April 1, I reconsider.")

## Examples

### Good Decision (Coherent Framework)

**Decision**: Accept job offer from StarkTech or stay at CurrentCorp?

**Weighted Criteria**:
- Salary (20%): StarkTech $165k, CurrentCorp $140k → StarkTech 9/1.8 vs 5/1.0
- Growth Potential (30%): StarkTech 9/2.7 vs CurrentCorp 6/1.8
- Team (15%): StarkTech 7/1.05 vs CurrentCorp 8/1.2
- Work-Life Balance (20%): StarkTech 5/1.0 vs CurrentCorp 8/1.6
- Equity Upside (15%): StarkTech 8/1.2 vs CurrentCorp 1/0.15

**Matrix Winner**: StarkTech (7.75 vs 5.75)

**Regret Test**: "Will I regret not staying at CurrentCorp? Only if the startup fails and I'm job hunting in 2 years. But I've wanted startup experience for 5 years. If I don't do it now at 32, I may never forgive myself."

**10-10-10**:
- 10 min: Nervous but excited
- 10 months: "Hope the startup is still solvent and I'm learning fast"
- 10 years: "Proud I took the bet, learned at a high-growth company, and either got equity upside or built reputation for next role"

**Decision**: Accept StarkTech

### Bad Decision (Missing Framework)

**Decision**: "I think I'll take the offer. My gut says it's right."

Problems:
- No criteria defined. Gut feeling is not reasoning.
- No weights. What matters and what doesn't?
- No regret test. What if it's wrong?
- No contingency. When would you reconsider?

## Common Mistakes (3+)

1. **Weighting Inflation**: You assign 10 criteria, all weighted 10% each (= 100%), so nothing differentiates. Cut to 5-7 criteria that actually separate the options.

2. **Gut Veto After Matrix**: The matrix says A, but your gut says B. You choose B. This isn't intuition; it's that your weights were wrong. Revisit weights instead of overriding the system.

3. **Missing the Trade-Off**: Two options have similar scores because they trade off differently. You pretend there's a clear winner. Honest decision-making acknowledges trade-offs explicitly.

4. **Regret Asymmetry Ignored**: You'd deeply regret not choosing option B, but you're leaning toward A. That's a signal. Investigate whether your criteria or weights need adjustment.

5. **No Contingency**: You decide but don't specify when/how you'd revisit. A month later, new information emerges, and you second-guess. Establish a decision window upfront: "If Y happens by [date], I reconsider. Otherwise, I'm committed."

## Anti-Patterns (3+)

1. **Decision Perfectionism**: Waiting for perfect information or a risk-free option. No such thing exists. If you've applied the frameworks and your confidence is medium, decide and move. You'll learn more from action than deliberation.

2. **Overthinking the Runner-Up**: You choose A but spend weeks second-guessing B. If you've done the frameworks and A scored higher, trust the system. Revisit only if new information emerges.

3. **Values Mismatch Hidden**: You choose based on salary (the matrix winner) but your core value is autonomy. Weeks later, you realize you chose against yourself. Do the values alignment step *before* deciding, not after.

4. **Delegating the Hard Part**: "What would you do?" is asking someone else to make your decision. Use frameworks to inform your choice; don't outsource ownership.

5. **No Sunset on Deliberation**: You keep adding criteria, re-weighting, and re-scoring. At some point, marginal deliberation reduces confidence, not increases it. Set a deadline: "I'll decide by Friday," then apply frameworks within that window.
