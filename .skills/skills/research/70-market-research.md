# Comprehensive Market Research

## Purpose
Market research synthesizes data on market size, customer behavior, competitive positioning, and industry trends to support strategic decisions. It translates business questions into research designs, collects primary and secondary data, analyzes market structure and dynamics, and produces actionable insights on addressable market opportunity, customer needs, and competitive advantage. Unlike desk research alone, rigorous market research integrates quantitative sizing with qualitative understanding of customer motivations, decision-making processes, and unmet needs.

## When to Use
- Validating market opportunity before entering new market segments
- Understanding customer needs, preferences, and decision criteria
- Sizing Total Addressable Market (TAM) and Service Addressable Market (SAM)
- Analyzing competitive positioning and differentiation opportunities
- Identifying market trends and shifts in customer behavior
- **Do NOT use when**: Only need quick competitive overviews, exploring internal capability assessments, or conducting product feature testing (use user research instead)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Define Market Research Questions and Success Metrics
Translate business strategy into specific research objectives. Don't ask "What's the market?" but rather:
- What segments have highest purchase intent?
- How do customer decision criteria vary by segment?
- What price sensitivity exists and where are willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds?
- Which competitors capture customers in priority segments?
- What unmet needs exist that we could address?

Document 3-5 core research questions. Define success metrics: "If we learn X, we'll go forward; if we learn Y, we'll pivot." Create research hypothesis framework: what do you believe now vs. what you need to validate?

### Step 2: Size Market Opportunity (TAM/SAM/SOM)
Calculate **Total Addressable Market (TAM)** — total revenue available to all providers in a market category. Use multiple approaches for triangulation:

**Top-down TAM**: Total addressable population × average purchase price × adoption rate. Example: For enterprise SaaS: 10M eligible companies × $5,000 avg annual spend × 15% adoption = $7.5B TAM.

**Bottom-up TAM**: Sum revenue across identified segments. Example: Enterprise segment ($2.5B) + Mid-market ($1.8B) + Small business ($0.4B) = $4.7B TAM.

**Value-based TAM**: Customer problem cost × customers affected × willingness to pay for solution. Example: Time wasted in process = 100 hours/year per employee × 500M office workers × $50/hour = $2.5 trillion; realistic addressable = $10B.

Calculate **Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM)** — realistic portion you can reach with current go-to-market (geography, channels, products). SAM = TAM × (% you can realistically address). If TAM is $10B but you target only US mid-market, SAM might be $800M.

Calculate **Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM)** — realistic market share in 3-5 years. SOM = SAM × realistic market share. If SAM is $800M and you aim for 5% share, SOM = $40M.

Document assumptions at each stage. Conduct sensitivity analysis: if adoption rates are 20% instead of 15%, how does TAM change? Use ranges (conservative/base/optimistic) not point estimates.

### Step 3: Segment Market and Prioritize Segments
Identify 4-6 market segments using meaningful criteria:
- **Customer characteristics**: Company size, industry, geography, purchase behavior
- **Need-based segmentation**: Customer problems solved, job-to-be-done
- **Behavioral segmentation**: Purchase frequency, channel preference, switching patterns
- **Psychographic**: Values, risk tolerance, sophistication

For each segment, calculate: Size (# of customers, revenue opportunity), growth rate, willingness-to-pay, purchase frequency, competition intensity, regulatory complexity. Create segment sizing summary with revenue estimates and growth projections.

Prioritize 2-3 segments for deeper analysis using criteria: size, growth, accessible-to-reach (ease of customer acquisition), profitable-for-you (good unit economics). Document reasoning for segment prioritization.

### Step 4: Conduct Primary Research on Customer Insights
Design research to understand customer needs, decision-making, and pain points. Methods include:

**In-depth interviews** (15-25 per segment): 30-60 minute conversations exploring: What problems do you solve? How do you currently solve them? What's missing? Who influences your decision? What's your budget? Document verbatim insights; identify patterns across interviews. Transcribe and code by theme (problems, workarounds, decision criteria, budget constraints).

**Online surveys** (100-500 per segment): Structured questionnaire measuring: problem importance (1-10 scale), current solutions used, satisfaction with current solutions, purchase criteria ranking, willingness-to-pay ranges, purchase timeline. Include open-ended questions for context. Analyze frequency distributions, correlations between problem importance and purchase intent.

**Focus groups** (4-6 per segment, 2-3 groups): Moderated group discussions revealing group dynamics, consensus on issues, reactions to concepts/pricing. Less reliable than 1:1 interviews for identifying true individual preferences (groupthink bias).

**Customer observation**: Shadow customers using current solutions; identify pain points, workarounds, time spent. Non-verbal observations reveal frustrations surveys miss.

**Jobs-to-be-done interviews**: Ask "What job were you hiring our product to do?" Understand functional job (complete task), emotional job (feel competent), social job (image, status). Identify competing alternatives (including doing nothing).

Minimum 50 qualitative interviews per segment to reach saturation (no new themes emerging).

### Step 5: Analyze Competitive Landscape
Map competitors across 2-3 dimensions (typically: price vs. feature richness; or: enterprise vs. SMB; or: on-premise vs. cloud). Create competitive positioning matrix showing:

**Direct competitors**: Functionally equivalent, overlapping customer segments. Document: pricing (features included), customer base size (if available), market share, funding/resources, product roadmap (from announcements), customer sentiment (reviews, NPS if available), differentiation claims.

**Indirect competitors**: Different solutions to same customer problem (e.g., outsourcing instead of software). Document threat level, switching barriers from indirect to your solution.

**White space**: Unmet customer needs not addressed by competitors. Example: Fast implementation + ease-of-use gap if all competitors require 6-month implementation.

Create competitive scorecard rating 6-8 competitors on 8-10 dimensions (price, ease of use, feature breadth, customer support, time-to-value, security, integrations, compliance). Identify differentiation opportunities: dimensions where customers value highly but competitors underdeliver, AND you can deliver.

### Step 6: Analyze Market Trends and Growth Drivers
Identify 3-5 macro trends reshaping the market:
- **Technology trends**: AI adoption, cloud migration, automation — how does trend expand your addressable market or create new needs?
- **Regulatory trends**: GDPR, data privacy laws — do compliance costs create new customer segments seeking compliant solutions?
- **Economic trends**: Inflation, recession risk — affects customer budget availability, ROI requirements
- **Customer demographic trends**: Remote work, generational shifts — change customer location, preferences, tools adoption
- **Industry-specific trends**: Digital transformation in healthcare, sustainability focus in manufacturing — affect what problems customers prioritize

Rate trend certainty (highly likely vs. speculative) and market impact (high/medium/low). Project market growth over 3-5 years: stable/declining markets vs. 20%+ annual growth markets.

### Step 7: Assess Market Maturity and Dynamics
Determine market lifecycle stage:
- **Emerging**: <$500M TAM, immature customer base, high growth (>30% YoY), few competitors. Challenges: customer education, funding competition, undefined standards
- **Growth**: $500M-$5B TAM, customer base expanding, growth 20-30% YoY, moderating to competitive intensity rising. Opportunities: segment specialization, platform expansion
- **Mature**: $5B+ TAM, growth <15% YoY, consolidation through M&A, competition on efficiency/service. Challenges: margin pressure, differentiation harder
- **Declining**: Shrinking TAM, customers switching to superior alternatives or solving differently. Challenges: unit economics worse, difficult to raise capital

Analyze market dynamics: Is consolidation occurring (fewer larger players)? Are new entrants disrupting? Are customer switching costs rising (platform lock-in) or falling (cloud commoditization)? What creates competitive advantage — brand, scale, technology, relationships?

### Step 8: Identify Unmet Needs and Opportunity Gaps
From interviews, compare current customer solutions to ideal state:

**Underserved segments**: Which customer types are getting poor service from competitors? Example: SMBs finding enterprise software too complex/expensive; if competitors underinvest in SMB UX, this is an opportunity.

**Unmet functional needs**: What tasks do customers accomplish inefficiently? Example: Customer spends 10 hours/week on manual data entry across disconnected systems; integration capability meets unmet need.

**Unmet emotional needs**: Do customers feel unsupported? Abandoned post-sale? Distrusted? Some competitors compete on superior customer success, brand trust, or customer advocacy.

**Future needs from trends**: As markets evolve (AI adoption, regulatory shifts), what new needs emerge that competitors haven't addressed?

Document opportunity score: (Problem frequency × problem severity × customer willingness-to-pay × competitive underservice) = opportunity attractiveness.

### Step 9: Gather Secondary Research Data
Supplement primary research with secondary sources:
- **Industry reports**: Gartner, IDC, Forrester reports on market size, growth rates, competitive positioning
- **Public company data**: SEC filings (10-K, 10-Q) of public competitors reveal revenue, growth, customer segments, margins
- **Trade publications**: Industry journals and newsletters document market trends, customer adoption patterns, regulatory changes
- **Analyst reports**: Equity research on public companies, venture capital trend reports
- **Academic research**: Systematic reviews of customer behavior, decision-making, market dynamics in your space
- **Government/statistical data**: Labor statistics, economic data, industry association reports

Document source date, methodology (survey? expert opinion?), sample size, confidence level. Secondary data is faster to access but potentially outdated or biased toward public information.

### Step 10: Synthesize Market Insights and Opportunity Assessment
Create comprehensive market assessment document addressing:

**Market opportunity**: TAM/SAM/SOM with confidence ranges; growth outlook; maturity stage. Why is this a valuable market?

**Customer segments**: Top 2-3 segments by size and attractiveness; characteristics; needs; decision-making process; willingness-to-pay; key concerns.

**Competitive positioning**: How do leading competitors position? What differentiation opportunities exist (white space)? What are competitors' strengths and weaknesses?

**Unmet needs and opportunities**: Top 3 unmet needs identified through customer research; frequency, severity, willingness-to-pay; competitive underservice; your ability to address.

**Market trends and growth drivers**: Key macro trends reshaping market; growth projections; implications for customer needs and competition.

**Risk factors and barriers to entry**: Regulatory complexity, customer switching costs, capital intensity, brand requirements, incumbent strength. Is market attractive or forbidding?

**Strategic recommendations**: Which segments to target? What differentiation to emphasize? What partnerships needed? What market validation should be done before major investment?

## Quality Gates

1. **TAM calculated using 2+ methods**: Top-down, bottom-up, and value-based methods employed; assumptions documented; sensitivity analysis shows impact of assumption variation on TAM
2. **Primary research conducted**: Minimum 50 customer interviews across target segments; jobs-to-be-done methodology applied; customer needs documented with verbatim quotes
3. **Customer segmentation explicit**: 3-5 segments defined using clear criteria (firmographic, behavioral, need-based); each segment sized in revenue terms; segments prioritized using transparent scoring
4. **Competitive analysis comprehensive**: 4-6 leading competitors analyzed; positioning matrix created; differentiation opportunities identified with evidence (customer needs not addressed by competitors)
5. **Unmet needs validated**: Top unmet needs identified by ≥30% of customers in segment; severity and willingness-to-pay demonstrated through direct customer evidence
6. **Trend analysis current**: Macro trends documented with evidence (analyst reports, company announcements, regulatory filings); projected market growth 3-5 years ahead
7. **Secondary research integrated**: Gartner/IDC/Forrester reports referenced; public company data reviewed; analyst perspective included
8. **Recommendations evidence-based**: Strategic priorities trace back to customer research findings and competitive analysis; not based on assumption
9. **Confidence levels documented**: TAM/SAM/SOM estimates include ranges and stated confidence levels; assumptions listed with impact if wrong
10. **Synthesis clarity**: Market assessment reads coherently; customer need → competitive gap → opportunity thesis flows logically

## Examples

### Good Market Research
**Question**: "Should we build industry-specific CRM for home services (plumbing, HVAC)?"

**TAM calculated**: 500K home services businesses × $2,400 avg annual CRM spend × 12% current adoption = $1.2B TAM. Bottom-up: plumbing ($320M) + HVAC ($280M) + electrical ($240M) + other ($360M) = $1.2B. Value-based: time saved per technician (8 hrs/week × 50 weeks × $45/hr) = $18,000 value; 500K businesses × 3 technicians average × $18,000 × 10% willingness-to-pay = $2.7B. Range: $1.2-2.7B; used conservative $1.2B.

**Primary research**: 60 interviews with plumbers/HVAC contractors; 200 online surveys. Key insight: 70% currently use spreadsheets or pen-and-paper (vs. 30% existing CRM users). Decision criteria: ease of use (9/10 importance), mobile functionality (8/10), low cost (8/10). Willingness-to-pay: $150-300/month for business with 3-5 technicians.

**Competitive analysis**: Examined ServiceTitan ($3.2B valuation, focused on mid/large contractors), HubSpot (generic CRM, not home-services-specific), Zoho (low-cost but requires tech expertise). Gap: underserviced small contractors (<5 technicians) who need simplicity + mobile + affordability.

**Recommendation**: TAM attractive; serviceable market in "small contractors, $150-300/month segment" = ~$200M. Primary opportunity unmet: ease-of-use + mobile-first for non-technical users. Recommend building industry-specific UX and templated workflows to win against generic CRMs.

### Poor Market Research
- TAM estimated as "Market research reports say $15B" without explaining methodology
- No primary customer research; recommendations based on "industry seems promising"
- Competitors listed (ServiceTitan, HubSpot) without differentiation analysis
- No customer willingness-to-pay validation
- Market growth assumed to be "30% annually" without evidence
- Unmet needs inferred ("They probably want mobile") not validated with customers
- No segment prioritization; unclear which customer to target
- Recommendations vague: "Build features customers want"

## Common Mistakes

1. **TAM inflated through aspirational assumptions**: "If 20% of home service businesses adopt within 5 years" is optimistic; assume realistic adoption rates (industry benchmarks typically 10-15% for B2B software). TAM used for 5-year projections shouldn't reflect unrealistic penetration rates. Solution: Use conservative adoption rates; separate TAM from SOM (what you can realistically win); document assumptions; conduct sensitivity analysis showing impact if adoption is 5% vs. 15%.

2. **Confusing market size with opportunity**: Large TAM doesn't guarantee business success if market is extremely competitive with low margins, high customer acquisition cost, or entrenched incumbents. A $1B market with 10 strong competitors may be worse than a $100M market with weak incumbents. Solution: Analyze competitive intensity, switching costs, customer acquisition cost relative to lifetime value; assess whether margins support growth investment.

3. **Insufficient customer research depth**: Surveys alone reveal stated preferences; interviews reveal actual behavior and unmet needs. "Customers say they want X" (survey) differs from "Customers currently do Y and are frustrated because Z" (observed behavior, interviews). Solution: Combine surveys with 30-50 in-depth interviews per segment; use jobs-to-be-done framework; observe customers using current solutions.

4. **Missing willingness-to-pay validation**: Assuming customers will pay based on value delivered (e.g., "saves 8 hours/week at $45/hour = $18,000 value") ignores that customers won't pay full value saved. Validate through direct WTP questions, conjoint analysis, or Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter. Solution: Ask directly: "Would you pay $200/month?" at multiple price points; triangulate with competitive pricing; don't assume value-to-price conversion.

5. **Competitive analysis without differentiation**: Listing 10 competitors without assessing how you'd win (what competitive advantage do you have?). Differentiation requires specific unmet need + your unique ability to address it. Solution: Create positioning matrix; identify 2-3 dimensions where customers rate competitors low but value highly; assess whether you can credibly deliver on those dimensions.

6. **Ignoring indirect competition and market alternatives**: Customers might not buy anything (do-it-themselves, outsource instead). Example: Software for IT automation competes not just with other software but with hiring contractors. If outsourcing is customer default, your marketing challenge is higher. Solution: Identify all alternatives customers consider; measure switching barriers and willingness-to-switch from alternatives; don't assume your market is only direct competitors.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Anchoring to initial TAM number without validation**: Estimating TAM early in research, then anchoring to that number even when customer research contradicts it. Example: TAM estimated at $5B based on analyst report, but customer interviews reveal willingness-to-pay is 50% lower than assumed, implying TAM is $2.5B. Sticking with $5B despite contradictory evidence. Solution: Calculate TAM before primary research; update based on validated customer data; document rationale for changes.

2. **Segment prioritization based on size alone**: Largest segments aren't always most attractive. Largest segment might be most competitive, slowest growth, lowest willingness-to-pay. Example: Mid-market segment ($200M) is largest but dominated by entrenched incumbent; small-market segment ($20M) is growing 40% annually with weak incumbent. Solution: Use multi-criteria scoring (size × growth × competitive intensity × WTP × acquisition cost) not size alone.

3. **Competitive analysis without customer validation**: Assuming competition gaps based on feature review. Example: "Competitor A lacks mobile app, so mobile is our differentiation." But if customer interviews reveal mobile isn't important (most office-based), you've identified a fake opportunity. Solution: Validate that customers value the dimension where you claim differentiation; measure customer dissatisfaction on that dimension; ensure competitors can't quickly add capability.

4. **Market trends extrapolated linearly**: Growth rates assumed constant when markets accelerate/decelerate. Example: "Market growing 15%/year so will be $5B in 5 years" assumes linear growth; inflection points (market adoption curves are S-shaped) and maturation aren't modeled. Solution: Model growth as S-curve with inflection point; research when market inflection occurred (if mature); use scenario planning (base/bull/bear cases) not point forecasts.

5. **Secondary research used without critical evaluation**: Analyst reports may be outdated, biased toward vendors they cover, or optimistic. Example: Using 2-year-old Gartner report without checking if market has shifted. Solution: Cross-reference multiple analyst sources; check publication dates; validate key claims with primary research; distinguish analyst projections from historical data; weight customer research more heavily than analyst opinion.

6. **Opportunity assessment without resource realism**: Identifying attractive opportunity but lacking resources to win. Example: "SMB segment is underserved" true, but going-to-market cost (sales, support, localization) may exceed profitability for low-ARPU segment. Solution: Assess opportunity attractiveness × your unique ability to serve; calculate unit economics (CAC, LTV, payback period) for each segment; prioritize segments where you have advantage AND profitability.
