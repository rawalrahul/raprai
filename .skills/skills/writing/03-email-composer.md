# Email Composer

## Purpose
Write emails that get read, understood, and acted upon. Email success hinges on: (1) subject line that doesn't get deleted, (2) opening that hooks the specific reader, (3) body that proves relevance, (4) CTA that is unmistakable and low-friction, and (5) tone that matches context and relationship. A good email reads like it was written for one person, not a template.

## When to Use
- Cold outreach to prospects, partners, or decision-makers (networking, sales, media pitches)
- Follow-up emails to non-responders or after meetings
- Negotiation, objection handling, or price discussion emails
- Apology or relationship repair emails
- Announcement, launch, or company news emails to groups
- **Do NOT use when**: Writing marketing campaigns (use copywriting-frameworks instead), automated systems that can't adapt to responses, or messages requiring legal review (contracts, compliance notices)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Identify Email Type & Recipient Psychology
Define the email type (cold outreach, follow-up, negotiation, apology, announcement) and the recipient's current state. Cold outreach: recipient doesn't know you, is time-starved, receives 100+ emails/day → must earn attention. Follow-up: recipient is familiar but hasn't responded → likely overwhelmed or not urgent → lower friction. Negotiation: recipient has position, ego, competing priorities → acknowledge their position first. Apology: recipient is frustrated or hurt → empathy precedes explanation. Announcement: recipient has varying interest levels → customize opening. This determines tone, length, CTA type, and opening hook.

### Step 2: Write Subject Line Using Proven Formulas
Subject line is the filter. Use these formulas (in order of effectiveness for your type): Pattern-interrupt (unusual claim or question), benefit statement ("+50% faster shipping"), curiosity gap ("Here's what changed"), personalization (name or specific detail), or urgency (time-limited). Keep under 50 characters. Test: would you open this? Avoid: "Just checking in", "FYI", "Follow-up", all-caps, excessive punctuation. Bad: "Let's chat about Q1 planning." Good: "3 ways to cut planning time by half" or "Sarah: Q1 planning shortcut inside."

### Step 3: Open with Recipient-Specific Hook (First 2-3 Lines)
Cold outreach: reference specific detail about them or their company (not generic flattery). Use name. Example: "I noticed your post on supply chain efficiency—we reduced lead times by 35% for similar retailers." Follow-up: reference the previous context. "Following up on our conversation about API integration..." Apology: lead with acknowledgment, not excuse. "I dropped the ball on the deadline last week, and I know that created issues for your team." Negotiation: acknowledge their position. "I understand the $50K budget is firm—here's why that might actually work in your favor." Announcement: lead with what changed and why it matters to them. Every opening should feel personally written, not templated.

### Step 4: Build Proof & Remove Friction (Body)
Keep body to 3-5 short paragraphs max (cold outreach, 2-3 only). Each paragraph = one idea. Cold outreach paragraph pattern: (a) proof you understand their situation, (b) what you're offering, (c) one data point why it works. Negotiation: (a) restate their position, (b) show you've researched alternatives, (c) propose mutual benefit. Apology: (a) specific admission of what went wrong, (b) impact it had, (c) concrete fix. Never lead with yourself; lead with them. Avoid: lengthy explanation, multiple ideas per paragraph, passive voice. Use short sentences: 8-12 words average.

### Step 5: Place CTA High & Make It Specific
CTA must be: clear (what exactly will happen next), low-friction (one ask, not multiple), and placed near end but not buried in a paragraph (own line or highlighted). CTA types: question (low pressure: "Does this sound worth 15 minutes?"), statement (direct: "Let's schedule a 20-minute call Thursday or Friday"), or micro-commitment (easiest: "Can you send me two dates that work?"). Worst CTAs: vague ("Let's chat"), multiple asks ("Call, email, or text"), or no CTA at all. Format CTA as its own short line or use bold. Example good CTAs: "Can you grab 15 minutes Wednesday?" or "Reply with 'yes' if you're interested."

### Step 6: Tone Calibration & Sign-Off
Tone must match relationship and context. Cold outreach: professional but warm, not salesy. Follow-up: casual-friendly but respectful of their time. Negotiation: confident but collaborative (not defensive, not aggressive). Apology: sincere and action-focused (not overly emotional, not deflecting). Review email for: (a) contractions (use them; "I'm" not "I am" = warmer), (b) exclamation points (max 1-2 total; too many = fake enthusiasm), (c) jargon (none, or define), (d) length (aim for 3-5 min read time). Sign-off matches tone: "Best," (casual), "Thanks," (friendly), "Cheers," (warm), "Sincerely," (formal). Never: "Sent from my iPhone" or multiple contact methods (one email, one phone only).

## Output Template

```
Subject: {{Formula-based subject line, <50 characters, passes "would you open this?" test}}

Hi {{First Name}},

{{Opening hook: 1-2 sentences that reference them specifically or acknowledge context. No fluff.}}

{{Body paragraph 1: What's the situation? Proof you understand their world.}}

{{Body paragraph 2 (if needed): What's the ask or offer? One idea per paragraph.}}

{{Body paragraph 3 (if needed): Why now? Data point, social proof, or mutual benefit.}}

{{CTA: Own line or highlighted. Clear, low-friction, specific.}}

{{Sign-off that matches tone}},
{{First Name}}
```

## Quality Gates

- [ ] Subject line under 50 characters; uses one of five formulas (pattern-interrupt, benefit, curiosity gap, personalization, urgency)
- [ ] Opening (first 2 lines) references recipient specifically or acknowledges context; uses their name
- [ ] Body is 3-5 short paragraphs max (cold outreach: 2-3); each paragraph = one idea
- [ ] Email reads like it was written for one person, not a template
- [ ] Average sentence length: 8-12 words; no paragraph exceeds 4 sentences
- [ ] Tone matches email type and relationship (warm-professional for cold outreach; sincere for apologies)
- [ ] CTA is specific, unmistakable, low-friction (one ask); appears on own line
- [ ] No jargon without context; no industry-speak that doesn't apply to recipient
- [ ] Zero passive voice ("I want to share" not "Sharing is something I want to do")
- [ ] Email feels earned, not intrusive; respects recipient's time

## Examples

### Good Output (Excerpt - Cold Outreach)

**Subject:** "Sarah: Cut planning time by 40% (seriously)"

Hi Sarah,

I saw your LinkedIn post about supply chain visibility and noticed you manage planning for about 50 SKUs across six distribution centers. That's the exact scenario where our team cut planning cycles from 6 weeks to 3.5 weeks for similar retailers.

We built a demand forecasting tool specifically for mid-market retailers (not enterprise, not DIY tools). It takes your existing sales data and cuts the guesswork by about 40%. Retail Dive covered it last month: saw a 23% improvement in forecast accuracy across the board.

Would you be open to a quick 15-minute call Thursday or Friday to see if it could help with your Q2 planning?

Best,
Sam

---

**Analysis:**
- Subject: under 50 chars, uses personalization + benefit (pattern interrupt)
- Opening: references her specific post + specific detail (50 SKUs, 6 distribution centers) = proves attention
- Para 1: proof of understanding + what's offered + data point
- Para 2: addresses her specific company size (mid-market, not generic)
- CTA: own line, specific (15 min, specific days), low-friction question
- Tone: professional but warm, conversational ("seriously", "the exact scenario")
- Length: 90 words, ~3 min read
- No jargon, no multiple asks

### Bad Output (What to Avoid)

**Subject:** "Exciting Opportunity"

Dear Sarah,

I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to you today to discuss a potentially great opportunity that could benefit your organization.

At our company, we specialize in supply chain optimization solutions that leverage advanced analytics and machine learning to streamline operations. Our comprehensive platform is designed to help businesses like yours increase efficiency, reduce costs, and improve overall performance.

We have worked with many satisfied clients in the retail industry, and we believe that our solution could be a great fit for your organization as well. We would be delighted to schedule a meeting at your earliest convenience to discuss how we can help you achieve your business goals.

Please feel free to reach out if you have any questions or would like to set up a call.

Best regards,
Sam Johnson
Director of Business Development
sam@company.com
(555) 123-4567
sam.johnson@linkedin.com

---

**Issues Identified:**
- Subject: generic, no specificity, clickbait-y ("Exciting Opportunity")
- No reference to Sarah's specific situation or work
- Robotic opening: "I hope this email finds you well" (ignored by 95% of readers)
- Body: all about the company ("our company", "our solution"), not about Sarah's needs
- Jargon overload: "leverage advanced analytics", "streamline operations"
- CTA: vague ("at your earliest convenience"), multiple contact methods (email + phone + LinkedIn)
- Tone: formal and impersonal, reads like template
- Length: 150+ words, 5+ min read (disrespects time)
- Passive voice throughout: "is designed", "could be a great fit"
- Multiple exclamation points (fake enthusiasm)

## Common Mistakes

1. **Mistake**: Opening with "I hope this email finds you well" or generic greeting
   → **Fix**: Open with a specific reference to them or their work. "I noticed your post on X..." or "Following up on our conversation..." Nothing generic.

2. **Mistake**: Making the email about you ("I'm reaching out because...", "We specialize in...")
   → **Fix**: Make it about them first. Lead with their situation, then offer your angle. "You're managing 50+ SKUs" (them) before "We built a forecasting tool" (you).

3. **Mistake**: Multiple CTAs or vague asks ("Let's chat", "Reach out at your convenience")
   → **Fix**: One specific, low-friction CTA. "Can you grab 15 min Wednesday at 2pm or 3pm?" is better than "Let's chat."

4. **Mistake**: Long paragraphs and long sentences that readers scroll past
   → **Fix**: 3-5 short paragraphs max for cold outreach; average 8-12 word sentences. Paragraph rule: if it takes more than 3 sentences to explain, split it.

5. **Mistake**: Formal, corporate tone that sounds like a template ("leverage", "optimal", "synergies")
   → **Fix**: Use conversational language. Read aloud. Would you say this in conversation? If not, rewrite. Contractions are your friend.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Never** open with flattery or praise if it's generic ("great company," "impressive work"). Specific observation only: "I noticed you wrote about X" not "You're clearly a thought leader."
- **Never** include multiple contact methods (email + phone + LinkedIn). One way to respond. Phone numbers make cold outreach feel pushy.
- **Never** write more than 150 words for cold outreach or 200 words for follow-up. Time-starved recipients won't read past paragraph 3.
- **Never** use ALL CAPS, multiple exclamation points, or emojis in professional emails. Undermines credibility.
- **Never** bury the ask. Readers scan email bottom-to-top sometimes. CTA must be unmissable (own line, clear language, high enough).
- **Never** reference a call-to-action tool, survey, or follow-up link that requires clicking; make the ask conversational ("Reply yes/no").
