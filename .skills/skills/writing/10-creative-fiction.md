# Creative Fiction Writer

## Purpose
Produce literary fiction (short stories, scenes, dialogue) that shows character through action and sensory detail rather than exposition. This skill enforces show-don't-tell mechanics, maintains character voice consistency between dialogue and narration, controls pacing through sentence rhythm, and injects specific sensory detail that elevates generic prose into compelling narrative.

## When to Use
- Writing short stories (under 5,000 words) with clear character arc
- Creating narrative scenes for longer manuscripts requiring consistency check
- Developing dialogue-heavy scenes where character voice must remain consistent
- Building flash fiction (under 1,500 words) with compressed pacing
- Writing first-person or close third-person narration with distinct voice
- **Do NOT use when**: creating plot outlines or scene summaries (use structural planning tool), writing dialogue-only scripts (use screenplay format), or producing children's stories with moralistic elements

## Instructions

### Step 1: Establish Character Voice and POV Consistency
Character voice must be consistent across dialogue, internal narration, and action descriptions. Weak voice consistency breaks immersion.

**Define character voice before writing:**
- **Speech patterns**: Does character use contractions? Formal vocabulary or slang? Short sentences or flowing periods? Internal monologue should mirror speech patterns.
- **Educational/class background**: Reveals through word choice, not statement. Working-class character uses "ain't," "real tired." Academic character uses complex sentence structures.
- **Emotional baseline**: Anxious characters use fragmented sentences and notice threats. Confident characters use declarative statements. Depressed characters use passive voice and flat affect.
- **Sense priorities**: Does character notice sounds, sights, smells, or tactile sensations first? Musician hears before seeing. Painter sees before hearing.

**Consistency tests:**

*Bad:* Character is "a lonely ranch hand with limited education"
- Dialogue: "This here opportunity seems propitious."
- Narration: "He contemplated the existential implications of isolation."
→ Voice is inconsistent. Vocabulary doesn't match background.

*Good:* Same character
- Dialogue: "This here looks like my shot."
- Narration: "The quiet stretched. He was used to that."
→ Voice is consistent: simple vocabulary, short sentences, concrete language.

### Step 2: Apply Show-Don't-Tell Ruthlessly
Exposition weakens prose; show character through action, dialogue, and response.

**Tell examples (weak):**
- "She was sad" → Show: "She stared at the unopened letter for twenty minutes, then filed it with the others, alphabetical by sender."
- "He was nervous" → Show: "His knee bounced. He checked his watch. Three minutes since he last checked."
- "They loved each other" → Show: "He made her coffee the way she preferred it (cream, no sugar). She left him notes in his jacket pockets."

**Formula: Replace emotional claim with physical response + specific action**
- Not "angry" but "jaw clenched, hands fisted"
- Not "intelligent" but "third person to solve the puzzle" or "caught the logical flaw others missed"
- Not "cruel" but "smiled while delivering the insult" or "laughed at someone else's expense"

**Dialogue reveals character without stating it:**
- Not "Tom was jealous" but Tom says: "I thought you were spending the night alone."
- Not "Maria was confident" but Maria says: "I've got this. Trust me."
- Not "Derek was dismissive" but Derek says: "Whatever, do what you want."

**Test every sentence:** If it tells an emotion or trait, rewrite to show action or dialogue.

### Step 3: Inject Specific Sensory Details
Generic sensory prose ("It was beautiful," "smelled good") reads as lazy. Specific details ground readers in scene and reveal character priorities.

**Sensory formula: Specific + Unexpected + Character-relevant**

*Sight (most-used sense):*
Bad: "The sunset was beautiful."
Good: "The sunset bled orange at the horizon, but up high the sky was still that industrial gray that made him miss Colorado."
→ Specific colors + character perspective (memories, mood)

*Sound:*
Bad: "The restaurant was loud."
Good: "Forks scraped plates at the next table. A laugh—hers?—cut through the ambient noise. His hand stilled."
→ Specific sound that triggers character reaction

*Smell:*
Bad: "The room smelled like old coffee."
Good: "Three-day-old coffee, the acid smell that meant the pot had been sitting since Tuesday. The smell of when she stopped trying."
→ Specific smell + character interpretation (shame, apathy, pattern)

*Touch:*
Bad: "The water was cold."
Good: "The water shocked his system—that gasp-inducing cold that kills the power to think, forces you into your body."
→ Specific temperature + physical impact + internal effect

*Taste (rarely used, high impact):*
Good: "The toast was burnt. He ate it anyway. The char caught on his tongue—the taste of mornings after fights."
→ Specific + action + emotional association

**Sensory hierarchy by character type:**
- Anxious character: notices threats (sharp sounds, closed spaces, exits)
- Sensual character: notices textures, tastes, smells
- Visual character: colors, composition, light
- Kinesthetic character: movement, rhythm, balance

### Step 4: Control Pacing Through Sentence Structure
Pacing is invisible but essential. Sentence rhythm controls reading speed and emotional intensity.

**Fast pacing (action scenes, urgency, dread):**
- Short sentences. Fragments even.
- Minimal adjectives. Active verbs only.
- No complex thoughts. One idea per sentence.

Example: "She ran. The footsteps behind her—closer. Breath ragged. She didn't look back."

**Slow pacing (reflection, beauty, intimacy):**
- Longer sentences with multiple clauses
- Descriptive language; complex syntax
- Internal observation; sensory lingering

Example: "She moved through the garden slowly, aware of each step, the way the late light filtered through the oak leaves and cast her shadow ahead of her like something leading her forward."

**Variable pacing (most effective narrative):**
- Short sentences to jolt ("He was gone.")
- Long sentences to linger ("The house held the heat of the day, that drowsy warmth that made thinking difficult, required nothing of you but your presence.")
- Mix short + long creates rhythm that prevents monotony

**Pacing tests:**
- Reread dialogue scene silently: does sentence rhythm match urgency?
- Reread description paragraph: does length and syntax match emotional tone?

### Step 5: Build Dialogue That Reveals Character and Advances Plot
Weak dialogue repeats exposition or states information. Strong dialogue does three things at once: reveals character, advances plot, and creates tension.

**Dialogue rules:**

No exposition dumping:
Bad: "As you know, we broke up three months ago because you wanted different things."
Good: "Three months. That's how long it's been since you decided we wanted different things."
→ Same information, but shows lingering hurt, passive-aggressive tone

No dialogue tags except "said":
Bad: "I can't do this anymore," she exasperated. "It's too much," he lamented.
Good: "I can't do this anymore." "It's too much."
→ Trust the words. Action beats carry tone: "I can't do this anymore." She stared at her hands.

Subtext (what's unsaid) carries meaning:
Bad: "I'm leaving you because I fell out of love with you."
Good: "I need to go." "When?" "Tonight." "...Okay."
→ The unsaid—all the reasons, all the hurt—lives in the silence

Character voice in dialogue:
- Formal character: complete sentences, few contractions
- Casual character: contractions, sentence fragments, filler words ("like," "you know")
- Anxious character: questions, qualifiers ("I think maybe," "sort of")
- Confident character: declaratives, no hedging

**Dialogue + action integration:**
Dialogue isolated on page feels flat. Pair with action:
"I'm scared."
vs.
"I'm scared." She wrapped her arms around herself.
vs.
"I'm scared." The word came out smaller than intended.

### Step 6: Structure Story Arc Within Scene
Short fiction requires compressed three-act structure: inciting incident, escalation, resolution/revelation.

**Act 1 (setup - 25% of prose):**
- Establish character, place, immediate situation
- Introduce one core tension or question
- Ground reader in sensory details
- No exposition; drop reader into action

**Act 2 (escalation - 50% of prose):**
- Develop tension; complicate the initial situation
- Reveal character through response to pressure
- Escalate stakes or emotional intensity
- Multiple beats of action/dialogue/reaction
- Sensory detail increases as tension increases

**Act 3 (resolution - 25% of prose):**
- Crisis point or decision moment
- Character choice reveals who they are
- Resolution (not necessarily happy, but definite)
- Final sensory detail or image (leaves lasting impression)

**Length mapping:**

1,500 word story:
- Act 1: 375 words
- Act 2: 750 words
- Act 3: 375 words

5,000 word story:
- Act 1: 1,250 words
- Act 2: 2,500 words
- Act 3: 1,250 words

### Step 7: Layer Thematic Resonance
Best fiction embodies its theme through action, not statement. Theme should emerge from character choices and sensory details, not author commentary.

**Identify theme as question:** ("What cost does ambition demand?" not "Ambition is destructive")

**Weave theme through:**
- Recurring sensory detail (light/darkness, temperature, specific object, texture)
- Character choice that embodies thematic question
- Dialogue that hints at the deeper question
- Resolution that answers the question through action not statement

Example: "What does it mean to keep a promise?"
- Recurring detail: Character checks watch, measures time
- Dialogue: "You promised." "I know." "Are you going to keep it?"
- Action: Character shows up at the appointed place, alone, waiting
- Resolution: Person doesn't come; character sits in silence, then leaves—but comes back next week

Theme is clear without being stated: promises matter even when unwitnessed.

## Output Template

### Short Story Structure
```
{{TITLE}}

[Opening: 1-2 sentences establishing character, place, tension]

[Scene setup: sensory details, dialogue, or action revealing inciting incident]

[Act 1 END: core question or conflict established]

---

[Escalation: character response, dialogue, complication, sensory detail]

[Escalation: raising stakes, pressure increases, character voice consistent]

[Escalation: crisis point approaches]

[Act 2 END: character must choose or respond]

---

[Resolution: character choice/action revealing character truth]

[Consequence of choice; final sensory detail]

[Closing: Image or line that resonates thematically]
```

## Quality Gates
- [ ] Character voice consistent between dialogue, internal narration, and action (speech patterns match vocabulary and sentence structure)
- [ ] No emotion or trait is stated ("sad," "angry," "intelligent"); all character traits shown through action/dialogue/response
- [ ] Every sensory detail is specific and character-relevant (no "it was beautiful"; every sensory phrase includes detail and perspective)
- [ ] Pacing matches emotional intensity (urgent scenes have short sentences and fragments; reflective scenes have longer syntax)
- [ ] Dialogue does at least two things: reveals character voice AND advances plot; no exposition dumping
- [ ] No dialogue tags except "said"; tone conveyed through action beats or subtext
- [ ] Story has clear three-act structure with inciting incident, escalation, and resolution
- [ ] Theme emerges from action/choice, not author commentary; thematic question answered through character decision
- [ ] Final image or closing line resonates with opening; creates sense of completion or revelation
- [ ] All background information earned through scene action, not exposition (no "As you know..." dialogue)

## Examples

### Good Output (Flash Fiction - 1,200 words)

```
The Wrong Train

She was three minutes late, which meant she'd missed the northbound entirely. The southbound pulled away from the platform as she descended the stairs, exhaust ghosting through the station.

The next train wouldn't come for eleven minutes. She checked her phone: 7:47. The appointment was at 8:00, thirty blocks north. She calculated: two minutes walking, six minutes on the train, maybe another minute finding the office. She'd be cutting it close.

The platform was empty except for an old man reading a newspaper folded to the crossword. She sat as far away as possible, habit more than anything. On weekday mornings, the southbound was packed.

Eight minutes.

At 7:55, she heard the low rumble. The train emerged from the tunnel. The doors hissed open. She stood, smoothing her interview jacket—navy, professional, the one that made her look older than twenty-six.

She stepped inside.

The train was half-empty. A teenager with headphones. A woman with three shopping bags at her feet. An older couple sitting close, the woman's head on the man's shoulder. She found a seat, closed her eyes.

When she opened them, she recognized the stop. Wrong direction.

She'd gotten on the southbound. Traveling away from the appointment.

For a moment she sat very still. The train slowed at the next stop. Doors opened. No one entered. The couple was still asleep or resting or whatever they were doing, unworried about directions.

She could get off, find a northbound train on the opposite platform, make up the time. She'd be late, but only by five minutes. Maybe ten.

She didn't move.

The train lurched forward again. They passed through dark tunnel for thirty seconds, then emerged into morning light. She watched the familiar streets scroll past: the bakery where she bought her father's birthday cake, the playground where she'd first met Marcus, the corner bodega that sold the good coffee for cheaper than anywhere else.

Her phone vibrated. Unknown number. She ignored it.

Seven more stops in this direction. Seven stops until the terminal at the southern edge. From there she'd need the opposite direction train. She'd be two hours late. Maybe three, depending on schedule delays.

She opened her phone, found the appointment confirmation: Dr. Sarah Chen, Reproductive Endocrinology, 8:00 AM. First consultation. Don't eat after midnight, bring insurance card, arrive 15 minutes early.

She had the insurance card in her wallet. Unused until now because the previous insurance had covered the 'before' part—the attempt part—and this new insurance, through the new job she hadn't started yet, covered the 'after' part. The fixing part. The moving-on part.

She'd practiced what she'd say. Yes, she'd tried for two years. Yes, there were no obvious problems. Yes, she was ready for the next steps.

Except she wasn't on the train going toward any of that. She was on the train going away.

The old man with the crossword was still there. She could see him from where she sat, one car over. His pencil paused. He was thinking about 47 Across, or 12 Down, or whatever small, solvable problem occupied his morning.

She could get off at the next stop. Figure it out. Go back.

At the next stop, she didn't move.

The woman with the shopping bags exited. An old woman got on with a walker, slowly, taking her time. The train lurched forward again. The couple woke up. They looked at each other, and the man pointed out the window at something she missed. The woman laughed quietly.

Two more stops until terminal.

Her phone vibrated again. Same unknown number. She knew who it was: Dr. Chen's office, calling to confirm she was on her way.

She silenced the phone.

The final stop came. She stood with the others as the train slowed, halted, doors opened. The couple got off. The old woman with the walker, assisted by a young man who appeared from nowhere—maybe her grandson, maybe a stranger with good instincts.

She was the last to exit.

The terminal was bright and empty. She stood still for a moment in the middle of the platform, surrounded by fluorescent light and the smell of metal and old concrete.

She could still make the appointment. It was 8:23. Wildly late. But possible.

Instead, she walked toward the exit stairs. Climbed them. Emerged at street level.

The city was awake now. People hurrying toward work, toward important things, toward the next steps. She walked in the opposite direction—toward the park she hadn't visited in five years, toward the corner bodega for coffee, toward the playground where she'd first met Marcus, who was probably with someone else now, someone with better odds.

The coffee was exactly the price she remembered. She sat on a bench overlooking the playground and drank it while it was hot. A child fell off the low climbing structure. Didn't cry. Got back up.

She watched that for a long time.

At 9:15, she texted the appointment number: "I need to reschedule."

The response came in four minutes: "Please call to rebook."

She put the phone away. She'd call tomorrow. Or next week. Or whenever the board in her head stopped spinning, whenever the train tracks that led away felt like a choice instead of a surrender.

For now, she sat in the morning sun, the coffee warm in her hands, and watched a child get back up from a fall.
```

### Bad Output (what to avoid)

```
Sarah was nervous about her doctor's appointment. She was running late and felt anxious about everything—the appointment, her future, her life. She'd been trying to get pregnant for a long time and it hadn't worked out, which made her sad and frustrated.

She got on the wrong train by accident. She realized this as the train pulled away. She felt stupid about the mistake. She thought about getting off at the next stop and going back, but she decided not to. She didn't feel ready for the appointment anyway because she was scared about what the doctor would say.

She rode the train in the wrong direction for a long time. There were other people on the train but she didn't pay attention to them. She was lost in her own thoughts, thinking about how her life wasn't going the way she wanted it to.

Finally, the train arrived at the terminal. Sarah got off and walked around the city for a while. She bought coffee at a bodega. She sat in the park and thought about her life. She decided to reschedule the appointment because she wasn't ready to face reality.

The end.
```

## Common Mistakes

1. **Mistake**: Stating emotions instead of showing them ("She was devastated") → **Fix**: Show through action, thought, physical response: "She stared at the door he'd closed. Twenty minutes. Then she picked up her phone, began a message, deleted it. Began again."

2. **Mistake**: Generic sensory description ("The room was beautiful," "it smelled nice") → **Fix**: Specific sensory detail tied to character or theme: "The light came through dusty windows, all copper and gold, and highlighted everything wrong with the place: the stain on the rug, the crack in the plaster. Beautiful and deteriorating."

3. **Mistake**: Dialogue that sounds identical regardless of character → **Fix**: Character voice should be distinct. Compare: "I cannot do this" (educated formal) vs. "I can't do this anymore" (colloquial) vs. "This is... I can't" (anxious, fragmented).

4. **Mistake**: Exposition dumping in dialogue ("As you know, we broke up last year because...") → **Fix**: Show the break-up through present tension and subtext, not explanation. Other character doesn't need to state what both know.

5. **Mistake**: Long descriptive passages that pause story momentum → **Fix**: Weave sensory detail into action. Instead of paragraph-long description, integrate: "She walked through the garden—roses overgrown, petals on the stone path, the smell of something dying—and sat on the bench."

## Anti-Patterns

- Never use dialogue to convey information the character already knows (breaks realism; readers spot exposition dumps immediately)
- Never write extended internal monologue explaining what the character should do (action speaks; thought should be brief)
- Never use more than one unnamed character in a scene (reader confusion; track everyone with names or distinct identifiers)
- Never tell the reader what something "was like" (just show it; trust imagery to convey experience)
- Never explain the theme (theme must emerge from action/choice; if you have to state it, it's weak)
- Never use exclamation points for emotional intensity (trust the words; exclamation points usually indicate weak diction)
- Never describe a character's appearance frontally ("She had blonde hair, blue eyes, was 5'7"...); weave into action or other character's reaction
- Never have characters speak in exposition-heavy sentences that no human would say aloud (read dialogue aloud; if it sounds wrong spoken, rewrite)
