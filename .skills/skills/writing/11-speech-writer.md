# Speech Writer

## Purpose
Produce speeches designed for human ears, not reading eyes. This skill applies oral rhetoric techniques (rule of three, callbacks, strategic pauses, rule of recency), narrative structure that builds toward payoff, and delivery mechanics (breath marks, emphasis notation, timing cues) that transform written words into memorable stage performance.

## When to Use
- Writing keynote speeches (15-45 minutes) for conferences or corporate events
- Creating toasts for weddings, events, or celebrations (2-5 minutes)
- Developing TED-style talks with personal narrative + universal insight
- Writing graduation or commencement speeches with aspirational messaging
- Building product launch speeches or investor pitches with persuasive architecture
- **Do NOT use when**: writing speeches for formal political events (require different rhetoric rules), creating written-only essays (use essay tool instead), or writing scripts for performers where delivery isn't your concern

## Instructions

### Step 1: Know Your Audience and Context
Speech structure depends entirely on audience, event type, and time constraint. Misjudging these breaks the speech.

**Audience analysis:**
- **Professional conference (business/tech)**: Expects takeaways. Open with problem/opportunity. Middle: evidence and case studies. Close: actionable framework or mindset shift. Skeptical audience; credibility matters more than emotion.
- **Graduation or life event**: Expects wisdom and inspiration. Opens with relatable failure or moment. Middle: journey or transformation. Close: aspirational call to action. Emotional audience; permission to be vulnerable.
- **Product launch**: Expects vision and urgency. Open with status quo problem. Middle: the solution and its impact. Close: rally cry and first action. Time-sensitive; pacing is faster than other speeches.
- **Toast (social event)**: Expects warmth, humor, and specific stories. Open with relationship claim. Middle: specific anecdotes proving the claim. Close: raise glass moment. Intimate audience; personal details over broad themes.

**Time constraint mapping:**
- 2-5 minutes (toast): 1 story, 1 joke, 1 heartfelt line, raised glass
- 10-15 minutes (TED-style): opening hook, one narrative arc, 3 key points, callback to opening, close with resonant question or statement
- 20-30 minutes (keynote): opening story, 3-4 sections each with evidence/narrative, multiple callbacks, strategic pause moments, closing that ties to opening
- 45+ minutes (extended keynote): opening story, 4-5 substantial sections with case studies, audience interaction moments, 2-3 strategic pauses for reflection, callbacks throughout, layered close

### Step 2: Open With Intrigue, Not Introduction
Weak openings introduce the speaker ("My name is...") or the topic ("Today I want to talk about..."). Strong openings hook the audience within first 20 seconds.

**Hook formulas that work on stage:**

*Startling statistic:*
"One of you will leave this job within the next two years. Maybe you already know that. Maybe that's the plan. But statistically, one person in this room is about to walk away from their career without saying why."
→ Creates personal relevance; audience leans in to figure out which one

*Specific story opening (not generic):*
Bad: "I once faced a challenge that changed my life."
Good: "I lost my job on a Tuesday. Not fired—laid off. Polite termination. They used the word 'restructuring' five times in a seven-minute conversation."
→ Specific detail (day, method, repeated word) makes audience believe story is real

*Provocative question:*
"How many of you have lied at work in the past year? Not a big lie. Not a fraud lie. Just a small lie. You know—'stuck in traffic,' 'email glitched,' 'I didn't get that message.'"
→ Rhetorical question creates shared vulnerability

*Contradiction or paradox:*
"The worst piece of advice I ever got was exactly the advice I needed."
→ Creates curiosity; audience wants to resolve the paradox

**Avoid:**
- Self-deprecating jokes (damages credibility from the start)
- Apologies ("I'm not really a public speaker...")
- Long, slow builds (you have 20 seconds to signal you're worth listening to)
- Thanking the organizers (put this in a note, not the speech)

### Step 3: Apply The Rule of Three
Rule of three is the most powerful structural device in public speaking. Three items feel complete; two feel incomplete; four feel excessive.

**Structure with rule of three:**

Pattern: Statement + Example 1 + Example 2 + Example 3 + Restatement

Example:
"Most companies fail at innovation for three reasons. First, they optimize for efficiency over experimentation. Google. Amazon. Adobe. All three got permission to waste resources on failed projects. Second, they measure too quickly. Netflix didn't expect streaming to succeed. They measured in years, not quarters. Third, they build culture around learning from failure. Airbnb early user data looked terrible. Instead of shutting down, they asked why. Those three things—permission to fail, patience to measure, culture of learning—separate innovators from everyone else."

**Rule of three in micro-scale (single sentences):**
- Three adjectives: "The idea was bold, specific, and achievable."
- Three actions: "Read widely. Write often. Share fearlessly."
- Three contrasts: "Slow thinking beats fast opinions. Deep work beats shallow effort. Patience beats urgency."

**Why three works neurologically:**
- One: too simple (audience questions credibility)
- Two: incomplete (brain waits for third)
- Three: pattern complete (brain satisfies and remembers)
- Four+: too many (audience stops tracking)

### Step 4: Use Callbacks for Coherence and Payoff
Callbacks (returning to earlier material) make speeches feel unified and reward audience attention. They're the main difference between speech and essay.

**Callback structure:**

Opening mentions detail: "I lost my job on a Tuesday."

Middle: (story of job loss, recovery, lessons)

Callback moment: "It's Thursday now. Not the same Thursday—this is sixteen months later. And I have that job back, but as a consultant, advising others through the same Tuesday I experienced."

Final callback in close: "So when you're waiting for your own Tuesday, remember: Tuesday is just a day. It's what you do on Wednesday that matters."

**Effective callback targets:**
- Opening image or metaphor: Open with "the path was dark." Callback midway: "I found a light." Close with: "the path is still dark, but now I carry the light."
- Repeated phrase: Use phrase three times, spaced throughout. Final use lands with different meaning.
- Character introduction: Introduce a specific person at opening. Bring them back with new information at close.
- Audience shared experience: "You all know this building." Use building as metaphor/anchor. Return to it.

**Callback formula:**
```
Audience hears line 1 (context, setup)
↓
Audience forgets about it
↓
Audience hears related line or situation in middle
↓
Audience consciously recognizes connection
↓
Audience nods/smiles (recognition creates pleasure)
```

### Step 5: Build Narrative Arc With Tension
Narrative arc keeps audience engaged; it's not plot but emotional trajectory.

**Narrative arc for speeches:**

Opening (30 seconds): Establish character (you), place (context), problem (tension)
- "I was standing in the airport, carry-on bag in hand, about to get on a plane I didn't want to catch."

Rising action (first half): Develop the tension, show struggle
- "The project had failed. Spectacularly. Public failure. Everyone in my company knew it was my idea, my execution, my fault."

Climactic moment (midpoint): The moment of truth or turning point
- "My boss called me into her office. I thought this was the firing meeting. It was the opposite."

Resolution (final quarter): Show consequences and transformation
- "She didn't want to fire me. She wanted to understand why I'd failed, and how I'd fix it. That question changed everything."

Closing (15-30 seconds): Land the insight and connect to audience
- "The question she asked is the question I ask every team I lead: how do we fail better? And that's what I'm here to teach you today."

### Step 6: Embed Audience Engagement Beats
Long speeches without interaction fatigue audiences. Strategic pauses, audience participation, and direct address keep engagement high.

**Audience engagement techniques:**

*Rhetorical questions (no answer expected):*
"How many of you have worked on something for a year only to watch it fail? [Pause. Let hands go up.] Right? Almost everyone."

*Pause after provocative statement:*
"The best leaders I know were fired at least once. [LONG PAUSE - 3 seconds feels like eternity on stage but is necessary] That's not common. That's not comfortable. But it's true."

*Direct address to specific groups:*
"For those of you in engineering, this next bit is for you. For those in sales, hold tight. [Then deliver section relevant to each] Now, everyone together..."

*Call and response (lite version):*
"Repeat after me silently in your head: 'I am capable of more than I believe.'"
→ Doesn't require shouting back; creates internal participation

*Invitation to reflection:*
"Think about the person who taught you the most. Not necessarily in school. In life. Got them? Hold that thought."

**Strategic pause rules:**
- After provocative statement: pause 2-3 seconds
- After key point: pause 1-2 seconds
- After question: pause 3-5 seconds (let audience think)
- Never fill silence with "um" or "like"—silence shows confidence

### Step 7: Write in Oral Rhythm, Not Written Syntax
Speeches read aloud need different grammar than essays. Shorter sentences. Fragments. Repetition that feels natural spoken, not stuffy on page.

**Oral vs. written:**

Written: "The intersection of technology and humanity represents the most significant opportunity for organizational transformation."
Oral: "Tech and people together. That's where the breakthrough happens."

Written: "We must consider the multifaceted implications of this decision."
Oral: "Think about what this means. For your team. For your customers. For you."

**Oral writing rules:**
- Sentences should be 8-12 words on average (longer = audience loses thread)
- Use fragments deliberately: "Silence. Then someone spoke."
- Repetition is your friend: "Slow down. That's not a suggestion. That's the insight."
- Active voice only: "We must change," not "change must be made"
- Use "you" and "we" constantly: creates intimacy, shared responsibility

**Test readability aloud:**
- Read paragraph aloud at natural speaking pace
- Mark places where you naturally pause (breathe there in written speech)
- Mark emphasis points with CAPS or underline
- If you stumble over phrasing, rewrite simpler

### Step 8: Construct the Close for Lasting Impact
Closing is what audience remembers. Weak closes waste a good speech.

**Close formulas:**

*Return to opening metaphor + full circle:*
Opening: "I was afraid to board that plane."
Close: "That plane landed. I'm still nervous about the next one. But I'm boarding it. And you should too."

*Restate core message with elevated language:*
Core: "Fail better."
Close: "Every failure is an invitation to become better than you were yesterday."

*Direct call to action (specific, not vague):*
Bad: "Go out there and change the world."
Good: "When you leave here, pick one way you're going to fail differently this week. Write it down. Tell someone. Do it."

*Raise glasses (for toasts):*
"To [person], who taught me that [lesson]. I hope we can teach each other something new this year. Cheers."

**Close dos and don'ts:**
- Do end with something quotable or memorable (audience will repeat it)
- Do tie to opening (creates satisfying circle)
- Do include concrete call to action (not "think about this" but "do this")
- Don't introduce new information (audience is checking out; solidify what they know)
- Don't apologize or thank people (wrong place; undercuts power)
- Don't end with a joke (unless you're a comedian; lands weak)

### Step 9: Notate for Delivery
Written speech is blueprint; delivery notation helps speaker execute with power and presence.

**Delivery notation symbols:**

[PAUSE 2 SEC] = indicated pause length for pacing
**EMPHASIS** = word or phrase to stress (not shouted, just vocal emphasis)
*slow down* = reduce pace for clarity or weight
*speed up* = increase pace for energy or urgency
[direct eye contact] = note to make eye contact with audience
[step forward] = physical movement to signal intensity
[breathe] = breath marker for natural rest point

**Example with notation:**
"The question she asked is the question I ask every team I lead: [PAUSE 1 SEC] how do we **fail better**? [PAUSE 2 SEC - long breath here] And that's [step forward] what I'm here to teach you today."

## Output Template

### Speech Structure
```
---OPENING (30-90 seconds)---
[Hook: specific story, statistic, or question]
[PAUSE]
[Introduce character/problem/context]

---RISING ACTION (30-50% of time)---
[Section 1 - First point with narrative evidence]
[Callback opportunity planted]
[PAUSE after key statement]

[Section 2 - Second point]
[Develop tension or complexity]

[Section 3 - Third point (rule of three)]
[Build to climactic moment]

---CLIMAX (brief, concentrated moment)---
[Turning point or revelation]
[PAUSE]

---RESOLUTION (final 25%)---
[Show transformation or consequence]
[Callback to opening]
[Second callback - deepen meaning]

---CLOSE (30-60 seconds)---
[Final callback tying to opening]
[Restate core insight with elevated language]
[Call to action - specific, not vague]
[Final image/thought for memory]
```

## Quality Gates
- [ ] Opening hook lands within 20 seconds without introducing self or thanking organizers
- [ ] Speech uses rule of three at least 3 times (in structure and in micro-statements)
- [ ] At least 2 callbacks present, with second callback adding deeper meaning than first
- [ ] Narrative arc clear: opening tension, rising action, climactic moment, resolution showing transformation
- [ ] At least 2 audience engagement moments (pauses, questions, direct address, or reflection invitations)
- [ ] Sentences average 8-12 words; no sentence exceeds 20 words without fragment variety
- [ ] All emphasis and pause points notated in text for speaker delivery
- [ ] Close ties to opening (full circle), restates core insight, and includes specific call to action
- [ ] Speech reads naturally when spoken aloud; no stumbling on phrasing when read at natural pace
- [ ] No vague language ("meaningful," "impactful," "transformative"); all claims are specific or supported by concrete example

## Examples

### Good Output (TED-Style Talk - 15 minutes / ~2,000 words)

```
THE QUESTION THAT CHANGED EVERYTHING

[OPENING - establish character and problem]

I was standing in the airport, carry-on bag in hand, about to get on a plane I didn't want to catch.

[PAUSE - let audience sit with that image]

It was Tuesday. [PAUSE] But more importantly, it was the day I would have to sit in a conference room across from 200 people I'd worked with for three years and admit: the project I'd spent that entire time building had failed. Spectacularly. Publicly. And it was entirely my fault.

The failure was beautiful in its completeness. We'd spent $2.3 million. We'd pivoted twice. We'd interviewed 500 customers. And the product we shipped solved a problem nobody had.

I was afraid of that plane ride. I was afraid of walking into that conference room. I was afraid my boss would fire me.

[PAUSE - long breath]

She didn't.

[RISING ACTION - Section 1: The Moment]

I walked into her office Thursday morning, expecting termination.

Instead, she asked me a question. One question: "What would you do differently?"

Not "Why did you fail?" Not "How could you waste this much money?" [PAUSE] Just: "What would you do differently?"

And I remember—I remember very clearly—my body changing in that moment. The tension that had been living in my shoulders for 72 hours just... [gesture] released. Because that question meant something: it meant she wasn't firing me. It meant we were going to learn something from this.

I spent the next two hours with her, not explaining why I failed, but understanding it. And more importantly, imagining how I'd approach it differently.

That question—"What would you do differently?"—became the thing I came back to again and again throughout my career.

[RISING ACTION - Section 2: The Pattern]

Here's what I noticed: the companies and leaders who asked that question—the ones who treated failure as data, not disaster—they iterated faster. [PAUSE] They shipped better products. They kept their teams intact instead of scattering after losses.

And the companies that didn't ask? [PAUSE] The ones that fired people after failure, or buried the post-mortems, or pretended the failure never happened? They repeated the failure. Different context, same mistake.

There's research on this. [PAUSE] Edith Schein at MIT studied how organizations learn. She found that organizations with high psychological safety—meaning people felt safe failing and discussing failure—those companies innovated 3.5x faster than organizations where failure was punished.

Three and a half times faster. That's not a marginal improvement. That's a completely different trajectory.

[PAUSE - let that land]

[RISING ACTION - Section 3: The Rule of Three]

So what does "fail better" actually mean? [PAUSE] I think it's three things. [PAUSE - audience knows rule of three is coming]

First: **permission**. Companies that fail better give people explicit permission to risk and lose. Not reckless permission. Smart permission. Amazon's two-pizza rule for projects. Google's 20% time. Airbnb's willingness to pay founders to iterate on product. These aren't nice-to-haves. They're how innovation actually works.

Second: **measurement that is patient**. Netflix's famous story about streaming: the data looked terrible for two years. But they measured in years, not quarters. They asked "Is this the direction we want?" not "Is this profitable next month?" [PAUSE] That patience is rare. That patience is competitive advantage.

Third: **culture of learning**. This is where most companies fail. They say they want people to learn from failure, but they don't actually create space for learning. No retrospectives. No honest post-mortems. No permission to discuss why. Just pressure to move on. [PAUSE] Moving on isn't learning. Moving on is just... moving on.

Permission. Patience. Learning. Those three things.

[CLIMACTIC MOMENT]

But here's the thing that most people miss: [PAUSE - step forward] You can have all three. And your team will still resist failure.

Because we're human. And humans are wired to avoid shame. And failure feels like shame.

So the thing my boss did that day—the thing that changed everything—wasn't any of those three things. It was something smaller and more fundamental.

[PAUSE - 3 seconds - long breath]

She was curious.

She asked the question like she genuinely wanted to know the answer. Not performative curiosity. Real curiosity. [PAUSE] And that curiosity gave me permission to be honest. Not just with her, but with myself.

[RESOLUTION - show transformation]

I left her office that Thursday and I did something I hadn't done in 72 hours: I breathed. [PAUSE] I called my team. We ran a retrospective, and I asked them the same question: What would you do differently?

The answers shocked me. They saw things I didn't. [PAUSE] Solutions I hadn't imagined. Approaches that would work.

We didn't save that product. It was too far gone. But we saved something else: we salvaged the learning. And that learning became the foundation for the next thing we built.

That product shipped 18 months later. It became our company's flagship. It's still used by 100,000 people today.

None of that would have happened if my boss had asked me to explain my failure instead of imagining our future.

[CALLBACK 1 - return to opening]

I did board that plane on Tuesday. [PAUSE] I sat in that conference room on Wednesday and told 200 people we'd failed. And instead of fire, I felt something else: possibility.

[CALLBACK 2 - deepen the meaning]

Here's what I want to leave you with: The question "What would you do differently?" is not a therapy question. It's a business question. [PAUSE] It's a leadership question. It's the question that separates companies that die and companies that learn.

[PAUSE - step forward slightly]

And more than that: it's the question that separates people who are stuck in their failure and people who move through it. [PAUSE] Every failure is an invitation to become curious about yourself, instead of ashamed of yourself.

[CLOSE - call to action]

So when you leave here today, I want you to do something. Don't think about something. Do something.

Pick **one** way you failed recently. Something that's still bothering you. [PAUSE] And ask yourself: What would I do differently?

Write it down. Not "I would try harder" or "I would be smarter." [PAUSE] Specific. Concrete.

Then tell someone. [PAUSE] Because here's the thing: shame thrives in silence. Learning thrives in conversation.

[FINAL IMAGE]

The plane landed. Tuesday became Wednesday. Failure became learning.

And that pattern repeated for the rest of my career.

I hope it can repeat for you too.

[PAUSE - hold, make eye contact]
```

### Bad Output (what to avoid)

```
Hello, my name is Sarah and I'm here to talk about failure. I want to thank the organizers for having me here today. Failure is an important topic that we need to discuss.

Failure happens when things don't go as planned. I experienced failure once in my career. It was difficult. I learned a lot from it. I think companies need to do better at handling failure.

There are several reasons why failure is important. First, failure teaches us lessons. Second, failure helps us grow. Third, failure makes us stronger.

Some research has been done on failure. Studies show that companies do better when they handle failure properly. One study found that companies with good psychological safety innovate more. Another study looked at Google. Google does well because they have good failure policies.

In conclusion, failure is important and we should all try to learn from it. Thank you for listening.
```

## Common Mistakes

1. **Mistake**: Opening with self-introduction or thanking organizers ("My name is..., and I want to thank...") → **Fix**: Open with hook (story, question, or stat) that signals you're worth listening to. Save thanks for written note afterward.

2. **Mistake**: Using rule of two instead of three (statement + example 1 + example 2) → **Fix**: Three creates pattern completion and memorability. Two feels incomplete.

3. **Mistake**: No callbacks; speech feels like disconnected essays strung together → **Fix**: Plant detail in opening. Return to it midway. Return again at close with new meaning. Creates unity and audience satisfaction.

4. **Mistake**: Writing like an essay (complex sentences, multiple clauses) instead of speech (short sentences, fragments) → **Fix**: Read aloud. If you stumble or run out of breath, rewrite shorter. Average sentence length 8-12 words.

5. **Mistake**: Closing with new information or "thank you" → **Fix**: Close ties opening, restates core insight, and includes specific call to action. Audience should remember call to action, not your gratitude.

## Anti-Patterns

- Never introduce yourself in the first 60 seconds (audience already knows who you are if they need to)
- Never ask questions you don't want answered (if you ask "Who's afraid of failure?" and one person laughs, you've lost momentum)
- Never tell a story and explain it ("That story shows...") - let audience infer meaning
- Never write speeches in passive voice ("mistakes were made," "consideration should be given")
- Never pause unless notation says to; awkward silence breaks flow if unjustified
- Never end with a question (statement lands harder than question at close)
- Never include more than one joke; comedic timing is hard and weak humor undercuts entire speech
- Never write the speech for reading; write for speaking (different grammar, different pacing, different power)
- Never use business jargon ("synergistic," "leverage," "circle back") - speak like human, not corporate
