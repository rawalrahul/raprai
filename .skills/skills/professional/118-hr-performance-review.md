# HR Performance Review

## Purpose
Performance reviews shape careers, retention, and promotion decisions. Strong reviews combine objective assessment (goals met, competencies demonstrated) with development coaching (where to grow, how to get there). This skill ensures reviews are evidence-based, fair, legally defensible, and genuinely useful for development. Combining feedback from multiple sources (self-assessment, 360 feedback, manager observation, peer input) creates a complete picture and increases buy-in.

## When to Use
- **Annual or semi-annual performance cycles** (typically November-January or May-June)
- **Promotion or high-visibility decision points** (when evidence of growth matters)
- **Course-correction conversations** (documenting performance concerns with specific examples)
- **Retention conversations** (articulating value and development path for key talent)
- **Do NOT use when**: Giving informal feedback (use quick check-ins), conducting exit interviews, or responding to disciplinary situations (which require different legal documentation)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Establish Review Cycle Context
Set the frame: Period covered, rating scale, how this review factors into compensation/promotion/merit.

Example header:
```
PERFORMANCE REVIEW
Employee: [Name]
Title: [Position]
Manager: [Manager Name]
Review Period: [MM/DD/YYYY - MM/DD/YYYY]
Rating Scale: Exceeds Expectations | Meets Expectations | Below Expectations | Needs Improvement
Purpose: This review informs [bonus eligibility / promotion consideration / development planning]
```

### Step 2: Conduct Self-Assessment Synthesis
Ask the employee to answer: What goals did you achieve? Where did you struggle? What do you want to develop? What feedback do you want to give about your role or manager?

In your review, synthesize their self-assessment: "In your self-review, you highlighted success on [goal], noting [specific metric]. You also identified [development area] as a learning edge. This aligns with [manager observation / 360 feedback]."

This shows you listened and positions the review as a conversation, not a judgment.

### Step 3: Map Goals to Business Outcomes
Review what goals were set at the cycle start. For each, assess:
- **Goal**: What was agreed
- **Status**: On track / Achieved / Off track / Revised
- **Impact**: Quantify the business or team outcome (revenue, quality improvement, launch, team impact)
- **Evidence**: Specific examples or metrics demonstrating progress

**Poor goal assessment:**
"Goal: Improve communication. Status: Partially achieved. Evidence: Feedback received."

**Strong goal assessment:**
"Goal: Lead design process for API v2 launch by Q3. Status: Achieved. Impact: Shipped 2 weeks early; customers report 40% faster integration. Evidence: Led 12 design sprints, synthesized feedback from 8 key accounts, mentored 2 junior designers through implementation."

### Step 4: Rate and Justify Competency Performance
Select 5-8 core competencies for your organization. For each, provide:
- **Rating**: Exceeds / Meets / Below (or similar scale)
- **Definition of the rating** (see Step 6 for calibration language)
- **Behavioral evidence**: Specific examples that demonstrate the rating

**Competency Rating Example** (Leadership):

**Competency: Leadership**
**Definition**: Influences and aligns team members toward shared goals; develops others; demonstrates accountability.
**Rating**: Meets Expectations
**Evidence**:
- Led team through 2-person surge (Q2 departures) without missing sprint commitments; provided coaching to junior engineer on technical decision-making
- Did not yet demonstrate peer-influencing at the organization level; feedback suggests reserving opinions in cross-team forums
- Sets clear expectations with direct reports; 3 of 4 report strong psychological safety and clarity

**Development Opportunity**: Increase visibility and peer influence by leading one cross-functional initiative in the next cycle.

### Step 5: Synthesize 360 Feedback
Collect feedback from 4-8 colleagues: 2-3 peers, 1-2 direct reports (if applicable), skip-level manager, and stakeholders. Synthesize themes (don't list all feedback verbatim; that's overwhelming).

Example synthesis:
"Peers and stakeholders highlight your technical depth and thoroughness. Multiple reviewers noted you're someone they trust to 'get it right.' Direct reports appreciate your mentorship and patience. Feedback also suggests an opportunity: in meetings, you sometimes hold back your perspective until others have spoken, which can slow decision-making. Consider sharing your thoughts earlier, especially when you have strong conviction."

This balances strengths with a growth lever and explains the "why" behind feedback.

### Step 6: Articulate Strengths With Specific Examples
List 3-5 key strengths. Each should include a concrete example, not a generalization.

**Poor strength section:**
"Strengths: Excellent communicator. Strong technical skills. Good teammate."

**Strong strength section:**
"Strengths:
1. **Clarity and Teaching**: Your ability to explain complex concepts makes you invaluable in onboarding and technical discussions. During Q2, you mentored the new engineer on database optimization; they went from struggling to leading our sharding redesign within 6 weeks.
2. **Reliability**: You deliver commitments consistently. You shipped the API logging enhancement on schedule despite scope creep mid-cycle, and customers cite the feature as a key improvement.
3. **Cross-functional collaboration**: You build trust across teams. Sales credits you with understanding their constraints; you've shipped features that balance technical debt reduction with customer requests."

### Step 7: Identify Development Areas & Growth Plan
Name 2-3 development areas. For each, define:
- **Area**: The skill or behavior to develop
- **Why it matters**: How it affects effectiveness or career growth
- **Current state**: Where the employee is now (with evidence)
- **Desired state**: Where they should be (ideally 12-18 months out)
- **Action plan**: Specific steps, resources, milestones, and accountability

**Development Area Example:**

**Area: Strategic Thinking and Long-Term Planning**
**Why it matters**: As a Senior Engineer, your promotion to Staff requires the ability to set technical direction beyond the current sprint. Leaders set strategy; individual contributors execute. You're strong at execution; stepping into strategy unlocks your next level.
**Current state**: You excel at shipping features once the direction is clear. You ask thoughtful clarifying questions but haven't yet led the vision-setting phase of a major initiative.
**Desired state**: By end of Q2, you're leading the architecture planning for our data pipeline redesign, owning the technical trade-off analysis and proposal to leadership.
**Action plan**:
- Enroll in [internal architecture course or external program] by end of Q1
- Partner with [name of Staff or Principal engineer] for bi-weekly 30-min mentoring on technical strategy
- Lead the data pipeline architecture working group; present findings to leadership in Q2
- Reflection checkpoint: mid-cycle check-in on progress and barriers

### Step 8: Create SMART Development Goals
Translate development areas into 2-3 formal goals for the next cycle using SMART framework:
- **Specific**: Exactly what will be delivered or learned
- **Measurable**: How you'll know progress (not just feel-good)
- **Achievable**: Realistic with effort and support
- **Relevant**: Aligned to role, career, and org strategy
- **Time-bound**: Clear deadline

**Poor development goal**: "Improve communication skills."

**SMART development goal**: "Lead technical communication for 2 cross-functional projects by Q2 2025, including architecture design docs and executive presentations. Success measured by peer feedback (360 survey) showing improvement in 'clarity of technical explanations' and 'visibility in decisions'."

### Step 9: Calibrate Rating Language
Consistent language across reviewers prevents bias and ensures defensibility. Define your rating scale with behavioral anchors:

**Exceeds Expectations**:
- Consistently delivers beyond role requirements in quality or scope
- Sets the standard; peers look to this person as a model
- Demonstrates the competency at the next level up
- Example: "Led 3 major initiatives; all shipped early and exceeded success metrics"

**Meets Expectations**:
- Delivers on all core responsibilities consistently and on time
- Demonstrates the competency as defined for the role
- Contributes to team goals; no significant gaps
- Example: "Shipped assigned features on schedule; quality is high; collaborates well with peers"

**Below Expectations**:
- Inconsistently delivers against core responsibilities or timelines
- Demonstrates the competency at a junior level; gaps are evident
- Requires coaching or support to meet baseline
- Example: "Shipped 70% of assigned work; quality issues required rework; needs closer management"

**Needs Improvement** (reserve for serious performance concerns):
- Fails to deliver core responsibilities; significant performance risk
- Requires immediate action plan and more frequent check-ins
- May warrant formal capability improvement plan (PIP)

Use consistent language: "Demonstrates", "Delivers", "Contributes", "Requires coaching", not personality descriptors ("seems smart", "has a great attitude").

### Step 10: Draft the Overall Assessment & Calibration Note
Summarize the performance story: What's the overall assessment? Is the rating justified by the evidence? Does the pattern suggest a trend (improving, stable, declining)?

**Overall Assessment Example:**
"[Name] has had a strong performance cycle. They met all core goal commitments, shipped high-quality features, and elevated the mentoring of junior engineers on the team. Feedback from peers and stakeholders highlights reliability and technical depth. Development opportunity: demonstrating strategic leadership (looking beyond the next quarter) will be key for senior-level growth. Overall rating: Meets Expectations with clear trajectory to Exceeds in the next cycle with focus on strategic initiative leadership."

**Calibration note** (for manager and HR):
"Rating: Meets Expectations is appropriate. Strong execution and impact. Not Exceeds because strategic portfolio ownership hasn't yet been demonstrated; with the planned mentorship and project leadership, expect promotion trajectory to Exceeds by mid-2025."

## Quality Gates

1. **Evidence Test**: Every claim (strength, development area, competency rating) includes at least one concrete example or metric. If a line has no evidence, it's not defensible; rewrite.

2. **Balance Check**: Does the review show a complete picture? Scan: Is it all positive? All critical? Most reviews are mixed (strengths + growth). If weighted too heavily one way, add nuance.

3. **Self-Assessment Loop**: Verify the employee's self-assessment is reflected in your review. "You noted [X]; that aligns with [manager observation]" creates psychological safety and shows listening.

4. **360 Synthesis Quality**: If 360 feedback was collected, are themes from multiple sources surfaced? Avoid: only citing one person's feedback or ignoring conflicting feedback. Synthesis means: "Most peers noted [X]; feedback from [person] suggests [different perspective]; we're observing [pattern]."

5. **Goal-to-Rating Alignment**: Does the overall rating match goal delivery? If someone crushed all goals but has an "Exceeds" rating, the story should be clear. If someone delivered goals but has "Below", flag the misalignment or explain the distinction (e.g., how they delivered matters, not just what).

6. **Development Plan Specificity**: Each development goal is SMART (not vague). Can the employee and manager agree on what success looks like by the deadline? If not, rewrite.

7. **Calibration & Legal Defensibility**: Could this review withstand HR or legal scrutiny? No personality judgments, no protected class language, all ratings backed by job-relevant behavioral evidence.

## Examples

### Good Performance Review Section

**Development Area: Influencing Across Organizational Boundaries**
**Why it matters**: As a team lead, your ability to influence engineering peers in other teams (not direct reports) directly affects project delivery and your readiness for senior leadership. Current gaps in peer influence slow decision-making.
**Current state**: You're highly effective with your own team but tend to defer to senior engineers in cross-team discussions, even when you have valuable perspective. In Q2's platform redesign working group, you held back your concerns about the proposed schema; peers later cited those same concerns as issues post-launch.
**Desired state**: By year-end, you're seen as a peer thought leader who contributes perspective confidently in cross-team forums and influences decisions without authority.
**Action plan**:
- Enroll in internal leadership workshop (Q1, $2K budget)
- Monthly mentorship with VP Engineering on influence and executive presence (starts Jan)
- Lead one cross-team architecture decision (Q2-Q3) end-to-end
- Peer feedback check-in (June): goal is 3+ peers noting "confident input in cross-team meetings"

**Why it works**: Clear reason (impact on job and career), specific current behavior with evidence, concrete future state, and measurable milestones with resources.

### Bad Performance Review Section

**Development Area: Communication**
**Why it matters**: Better communication will help you grow.
**Current state**: You could improve your communication.
**Desired state**: Communicate better.
**Action plan**: Work on communication skills. Ask manager for help.

**Why it fails**: Vague at every level. What communication? Which situations? What does "better" mean? No accountability or specificity.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Conflating personality with performance**: "You're too quiet" or "You're overbearing" are personality judgments, not performance feedback. Reframe: "In meetings, you contribute after others; try stating your perspective earlier, especially when you have conviction" (behavior-specific).

2. **Generalizations without evidence**: "Strong team player" or "great problem solver" without examples. Adds: "During the [project name], you synthesized input from 5 teams and proposed a solution that [specific outcome]. Peers credit your ability to find common ground."

3. **Comparing to other employees**: "You're not as senior as [colleague]" undermines fairness. Compare to role expectations and competencies, never to peers.

4. **Ignoring 360 conflicting feedback**: If 3 peers praise collaboration but the manager says it's weak, address the discrepancy: "Feedback from [person] and observations differ here; let's discuss." Ignoring conflict suggests you didn't take feedback seriously.

5. **Development areas as criticisms**: "You need to stop being so detail-oriented" comes across as punishment. Reframe: "Your attention to quality is a strength; channel it into architecture and process design [next-level use case] to increase impact."

6. **Rating without calibration**: Two managers rating the same outcome differently because they apply different standards. Mitigation: annual calibration meeting where managers present reviews and align on rating rigor.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **The recycled review**: Using boilerplate or previous-cycle language. Every review should reflect the specific period, goals, and circumstances. Employees notice and disengage.

2. **Surprises in the formal review**: If a "Below Expectations" rating appears in the formal review for the first time, the manager hasn't been giving clear feedback. Regular check-ins (monthly 1:1s) should flag concerns early.

3. **Compensation/promotion as afterthought**: Leaving compensation and career implications vague ("We'll discuss this separately"). Clarity at review time reduces confusion and resentment. If a rating determines eligibility for promotion or bonus, state it.

4. **One-size-fits-all feedback**: Treating junior and senior employees the same. A junior engineer needs more coaching and clarity; a senior engineer needs stretch challenges. Tailor development areas and expectations to level.

5. **Skipping the self-assessment synthesis**: Not referencing what the employee said they did. This signals the review is one-way (judgment from on high) rather than a conversation. Always synthesize their self-assessment; agree or respectfully disagree with evidence.
