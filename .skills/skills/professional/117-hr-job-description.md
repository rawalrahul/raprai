# HR Job Description

## Purpose
A strong job description attracts qualified candidates, sets clear expectations, and creates a defensible hiring record. Beyond listing duties, modern job descriptions signal company culture, growth opportunity, and commitment to inclusion. This skill combines legal rigor (avoiding discriminatory language, stating non-negotiables clearly) with marketing appeal (articulating mission impact, career trajectory). Candidates and hiring teams both benefit from clarity.

## When to Use
- **Creating a new open role** (greenfield hire or backfill)
- **Refreshing an existing description** (role has evolved; team feedback suggests clarity needed)
- **Ensuring compliance** (DEI audit, legal review, or preparing for hiring surge)
- **Expanding market reach** (optimizing for job board searches, geographic reach)
- **Do NOT use when**: Describing internal organizational structure for compliance docs (use org charts), creating executive-level biographies, or drafting offer letters (different format entirely)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Establish the Role Summary
Write 2-4 sentences that answer: What is the job fundamentally about? What will this person do day-to-day? Why does it matter?

Structure: `[Title] reports to [Manager] and is responsible for [core purpose in 1 sentence]. This role [key activities or impact areas]. We're looking for someone who [behavioral/mindset fit]. [Optional: growth trajectory or unique angle]`

**Example** (Senior Product Manager, Analytics Platform):
"The Senior Product Manager for Analytics owns roadmap strategy and delivery for our core analytics platform, which serves 2,000+ enterprise customers. You'll balance customer discovery, competitive analysis, and technical feasibility to ship features that drive retention and expansion revenue. We're seeking a product leader who thrives in ambiguity, builds cross-functional trust, and has shipped B2B products into regulated industries. This role offers clear path to Director of Product within 18-24 months for high performers."

### Step 2: Define Responsibilities (8-12 Bullets)
Use action verbs (lead, develop, analyze, mentor, design, scale, optimize). Avoid vague verbs (manage, handle, do, support). Start each bullet with a verb, not a noun.

Each responsibility should map to at least one competency (see Step 5). Vary the level of detail: some bullets describe scope, others describe typical projects.

**Poor responsibility bullets:**
- Manages reports
- Responsible for communications
- Handles budget process

**Strong responsibility bullets:**
- Lead quarterly business reviews with top 20 enterprise accounts to identify expansion opportunities; own $1.2M expansion revenue target
- Design and execute go-to-market strategy for new product features, including messaging framework, sales enablement, and success metrics
- Mentor 2-3 junior PMs on roadmap prioritization and stakeholder management; conduct weekly 1:1s and facilitate monthly product critique sessions
- Analyze competitive moves and market trends monthly; synthesize findings into strategic recommendations for leadership

Tip: Try the "if this person left, what wouldn't get done?" test. That's a responsibility worth listing.

### Step 3: Specify Required vs. Preferred Qualifications
**Required**: Non-negotiable baseline. Person must have these on day one or be able to acquire them within first 60 days with support. Keep this list short (3-6 items) to avoid disqualifying diverse candidates.

**Preferred**: Nice-to-haves. Often signal seniority, specialized knowledge, or cultural fit but shouldn't be gatekeeping.

Avoid years-of-experience rules ("10+ years"). Instead, anchor to demonstrated capability ("has shipped 3+ products to market", "has led cross-functional teams of 5+").

**Example**:

*Required:*
- 5+ years of product management experience in B2B SaaS or enterprise software
- Demonstrated ability to drive product strategy through ambiguous problems using data and customer insight
- Strong written and verbal communication; comfortable presenting to executive audiences
- Experience with SQL or analytics tools; comfortable diving into raw data to answer questions

*Preferred:*
- Background in data analytics, data science, or analytics platforms
- Experience in regulated industries (healthcare, finance, government) or compliance-heavy environments
- Prior startup experience or exposure to high-growth environments
- Familiarity with Agile/Scrum development practices

### Step 4: Add Competency Framework
List 4-6 core competencies required for success. Define each in 1-2 sentences so candidates self-assess and interviewers calibrate scoring.

**Example Competency Framework** (Product Manager role):

1. **Strategic Thinking**: Synthesizes market data, customer feedback, and technical constraints to articulate a clear product vision. Thinks beyond the next quarter; anticipates competitive and market shifts.

2. **Cross-Functional Leadership**: Builds trust and alignment across engineering, design, sales, and success teams without direct authority. Facilitates productive disagreement and surfaces data-driven decisions.

3. **Customer Empathy**: Deeply understands customer problems and business contexts. Conducts research regularly; can articulate customer value prop from multiple stakeholder perspectives.

4. **Analytical Rigor**: Designs experiments to test hypotheses; uses quantitative and qualitative data to validate decisions. Comfortable with metrics, SQL, and statistical thinking.

5. **Execution Discipline**: Manages roadmap trade-offs, tracks progress against milestones, and delivers updates to stakeholders. Balances speed with quality and adapts when plans change.

6. **Resilience & Growth Mindset**: Learns from failures; seeks feedback actively. Stays motivated when facing setbacks or ambiguity.

### Step 5: Conduct a DEI & Inclusive Language Audit
Review for language that may inadvertently exclude or alienate candidates:

- **Avoid**: "Native English speaker" (may discriminate against non-native fluent speakers). **Instead**: "Excellent written and verbal communication in English."
- **Avoid**: "Digital native" or "tech-savvy" (assumes certain background or age). **Instead**: "Comfortable learning new software and tools quickly."
- **Avoid**: "Aggressive", "dominance", "rockstar", "ninja" (coded language; can discourage women and underrepresented groups). **Instead**: "Drives accountability", "delivers high-impact results", "expert-level".
- **Avoid**: "Energetic", "young", "culture fit" (can trigger age bias or homophily bias). **Instead**: "Collaborative", "aligned with our values", "brings diverse perspective."
- **Avoid**: Requiring a specific degree if not legally required. **Instead**: "Bachelor's degree in relevant field OR equivalent professional experience."

Add a note: "We encourage candidates from underrepresented groups to apply. We are committed to building a diverse team."

### Step 6: Include Salary Transparency
Job posting laws in many regions (CA, NY, Colorado, UK) now require salary ranges. Even where not mandated, transparency improves applications and reduces time-to-hire.

Include: `Salary Range: $[Low] - $[High] based on experience, location, and qualifications.`

Add context: `We offer competitive benefits including [health insurance, equity, flexible work, professional development budget].`

### Step 7: Describe Career Growth & Culture
Candidates increasingly care about trajectory and working environment. Add 2-3 sentences:

- **Growth path**: "This role offers clear visibility to VP or Director-level opportunity within 18 months for high performers. You'll mentor junior PMs and shape product strategy as you grow."
- **Culture signal**: "We value transparency, psychological safety, and rapid iteration. Failure is part of learning here; we debrief after misses and adjust."
- **Unique angle**: "You'll work with a technical founding team that still codes. You'll learn world-class engineering practices firsthand."

### Step 8: Add Application & Next Steps Instructions
Be explicit about what you're looking for in applications:

"To apply: Submit a resume, cover letter (1 page max), and a link to your portfolio or 1-2 examples of product work (blog post, case study, or GitHub repo). Tell us: What's a product decision you'd make differently, and why?"

This filters for candidates who will invest thought and shows you want to understand their thinking.

## Quality Gates

1. **Verb Diversity Check**: Scan all 8-12 responsibility bullets. Do at least 8 different action verbs appear? If you see "manage", "support", "help" repeated, rewrite for specificity and variety.

2. **Required Qualifications Rigor**: Count the items. More than 7? Review each: Is it truly non-negotiable on day one? If not, move to Preferred. Fewer than 3? You may be too vague; add specifics on scope, impact, or experience level.

3. **Competency-to-Responsibility Mapping**: Each competency should map to at least 2 responsibility bullets. If a competency is listed but not evidenced in job duties, remove it or rewrite a responsibility.

4. **DEI Language Scan**: Search for: "native speaker", "culture fit", "rockstar", "digital native", "young", "aggressive", "energetic", "high-energy", "must have", or gendered language. Replace with objective, inclusive alternatives.

5. **Salary Range Transparency**: Verify a salary range or budget band is included. If prohibited by law in your jurisdiction, note that. If omitted, candidates hesitate to apply or negotiate unfairly.

6. **Readability & Length**: JD should be 1-2 pages, skimmable. If it exceeds 3 pages, consolidate. Use short sentences and bullet format.

## Examples

### Good Job Description Section

**Role Summary**: "The Staff Software Engineer for Infrastructure leads architecture decisions and mentorship for our platform team of 12 engineers. You'll design systems that serve 10M+ daily requests, mentor 3-4 junior engineers on design patterns and debugging, and unblock teams through technical problem-solving. We're seeking someone who combines deep systems thinking with patience for teaching and a bias toward shipping. This role is a clear stepping stone to Principal Engineer or Engineering Manager within 2-3 years."

**Why it works**: Specific scope (12-person team, 10M+ requests), clear impact (mentorship + systems), behavioral fit (patience, shipping bias), and transparent growth path.

### Bad Job Description Section

**Role Summary**: "We're looking for a talented Software Engineer to join our growing team. You'll work on cool tech and collaborate with other smart people. This is a great opportunity for someone who loves to code."

**Why it fails**: No specificity on team size, tech stack, or impact. "Talented", "cool", "smart people" are vacuous. No signal of growth or unique challenge. Could describe 1,000 jobs.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Inflating requirements**: Listing "10+ years" when the role is mid-level, or requiring a PhD when a bootcamp grad succeeds. Review internally: What's the minimum to succeed in the first 90 days? Only list that. High barriers reduce diverse applicants.

2. **Vague responsibilities**: "Support the team" or "Handle communications". These don't tell a candidate what they'll spend 40% of their time doing. Rewrite: "Lead weekly cross-functional syncs with product, design, and engineering (4 hours/week); document decisions and track action items using Slack and Notion."

3. **Burying the mission**: Generic JD that could describe any company. Add specificity: customer impact, market context, or unique challenge your company faces. Why should someone choose you over competitors?

4. **Missing growth trajectory**: Leaving candidates to guess whether this role leads anywhere. "Path to Senior / Director within 18-24 months" is concrete and compelling.

5. **No salary band**: Wastes time screening candidates whose expectations don't match your budget. Transparency saves everyone.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **The kitchen-sink JD**: Listing every task performed by the current team member, including updating the office supply spreadsheet and organizing offsites. Keep focus on 8-12 core responsibilities that define the role's value.

2. **Requiring specific tools**: "Must know Salesforce, Marketo, and HubSpot." Instead: "Proficient with CRM and marketing automation platforms; will learn our specific stack in onboarding." Tools change; adaptability matters more.

3. **Avoiding accountability language**: "Contributes to...", "Helps with...", "Assists in...". Candidates want ownership. Use "Owns", "Leads", "Drives", "Delivers". If the role truly has no ownership, reconsider whether it's a full-time role.

4. **Using the job description as a hiring rubric**: JD and interview questions are different. JD describes the job; rubric defines how you'll assess candidates. Some responsibilities (e.g., "mentor junior engineers") may not be testable in an interview. Separate these.
