# Case Study Writer

## Purpose
Case studies are your most credible sales tool: they prove your solution works in the real world. This skill structures a case study to maximize impact through three elements: (1) relatable problem identification, (2) specific solution implementation, and (3) measurable quantified results. A well-written case study converts skepticism into confidence by showing, not telling, how you deliver value.

## When to Use
- Responding to sales prospects who ask "Do you have examples?"
- Building portfolio materials for new market verticals
- Creating content for sales presentations and proposals
- Showcasing before-and-after transformations with metrics
- Developing case studies for LinkedIn articles or website case study pages
- **Do NOT use when**: you lack permission from the client, results are proprietary/under NDA (anonymize instead), or the timeframe is too recent to show sustained results

## Instructions

### Step 1: Identify Your Headline Result
Write a single, quantified headline that captures the core business impact. This should be the dominant headline above the case study and appear in your database title. Examples: "Reduced Call Volume by 40% in 6 Months" or "Grew Revenue from $2M to $8M in 18 Months."

The headline must:
- Lead with a number (40%, $6M increase, 3x faster)
- Be specific to this client's scenario, not generic
- Represent the topline business outcome, not a feature benefit
- Be provable with supporting data shown later

### Step 2: Build Company Background (150-200 words)
Establish credibility and context with:
- **Company name, industry, and company size** (employees/revenue). Example: "Acme Manufacturing, industrial automation, 250 employees, $15M annual revenue"
- **Market position**: public/private, founded when, growth trajectory. Is this a market leader or emerging challenger?
- **Why this company matters to your reader**: what makes this client relatable? (same industry, same size, similar competitive pressure)

Use specific details. Avoid: "large global manufacturer"—instead write "mid-market manufacturer in precision metalworking, competing with 3 larger publicly traded firms."

### Step 3: Define the Challenge (250-350 words)
The challenge section is the emotional core. Prospects read this thinking, "That's exactly my problem." Structure it as:

**The Specific Pain Point** (what was broken)
- Don't say "manual processes were inefficient"
- Instead: "Incoming customer service requests averaged 200/day. The team logged them manually into 5 different spreadsheets, creating duplicate work and data inconsistencies. Customers waited 72+ hours for a response."

**Failed Attempts** (what they tried before you)
- Show they weren't lazy or incompetent—they tried alternatives first
- Example: "Acme evaluated three off-the-shelf ITSM platforms in 2022. Two required 6-month implementations with major process redesigns. The third couldn't integrate with their existing accounting system. They shelved the project."

**Business Impact of Inaction** (why this mattered)
- Connect the operational pain to business outcomes
- Example: "This 72-hour lag resulted in 15% customer churn and $1.2M in lost annual revenue. The support team was working 50+ hour weeks, leading to burnout and staff turnover."

Use data whenever possible. If exact numbers aren't available, use reasonable estimates: "approximately 200 requests/day," "roughly 72 hours response time."

### Step 4: Present the Solution (250-350 words)
Detail what you actually built, not what your product does in theory.

**What Was Implemented**
- Specific tools/modules deployed (not product names, but actual configurations)
- Timeline and scope (phased rollout or big bang? 3 weeks or 12 weeks?)
- Integrations you built (if it connects to their existing systems, show this—it's differentiating)
- People involved (did a consultant help? Did your team embed with the client?)

Example: "We deployed the customer request management module with custom API integrations to Acme's existing accounting software (NetSuite). The implementation team spent 4 weeks on-site configuring workflows and training staff. Auto-categorization rules reduced manual data entry by 70%."

**Why This Approach**
- Show your thinking, not just your actions
- Example: "Rather than replacing their entire system, we created a unified intake layer that aggregated requests from email, phone, and web. This approach preserved their existing workflows while solving the fragmentation problem."

**How Clients Participated**
- Include their effort/buy-in
- Example: "Key stakeholders from customer support, operations, and IT participated in 3 design sessions to map optimal workflows before we built custom configurations."

### Step 5: Quantify Results (200-250 words)
Lead with 3-5 quantified metrics. Present them in before-and-after format:

| Metric | Before | After | Change |
|--------|--------|-------|--------|
| Average Response Time | 72 hours | 4 hours | -94% |
| Request Processing Time | 45 min/request | 3 min/request | -93% |
| Data Entry Errors | 15-20% of requests | <1% | -95% |
| Support Team Utilization | 50+ hrs/week (overtime) | 40 hrs/week | -20% |
| Customer Satisfaction (CSAT) | 62% | 89% | +27 pts |

For each metric:
- State it precisely (not "faster" but "72 hours to 4 hours")
- Include the timeframe (achieved within 6 months, sustained for 12 months)
- Connect to business value (4-hour response time reduced churn by 15%)

Include secondary benefits: "Support team no longer works weekends, improving morale and reducing turnover."

### Step 6: Include Client Testimonial/Quote (50-100 words)
Use a direct quote from a stakeholder with decision-making authority (VP, Director, CFO—not individual contributor). The quote should:
- Address the core business outcome
- Sound authentic (not marketing-speak)
- Include the speaker's name, title, and company

Example: "The implementation exceeded our expectations. We went from losing customers due to response times to being a differentiator in our market. In the first year alone, the solution paid for itself through reduced churn." — Jennifer Park, VP Customer Operations, Acme Manufacturing

### Step 7: Craft Call-to-Action
End with a specific next step, not a vague "contact us":
- "Schedule a 30-minute discovery call to explore similar results for your organization" (link to calendar)
- "Download our implementation checklist for [Industry] companies" (link to PDF)
- "See how [Product] reduced support response times for 15 mid-market manufacturers" (link to related asset)

## Quality Gates
1. **Quantified headline result**: Does your headline lead with a number (%, $, multiplier)?
2. **Relatable problem**: Can your reader see their own situation in the "Challenge" section?
3. **Specific solution**: Are you describing actual implementation, not marketing language?
4. **Before-and-after metrics**: Are results presented in side-by-side comparison format with timeframes?
5. **Authentic voice**: Does the client quote sound like a real person, not marketing?
6. **Business impact**: Does every metric connect to a business outcome (revenue, efficiency, growth)?

## Examples

### Good Case Study Introduction
"TechStart, a 120-person SaaS company generating $8M in ARR, reduced their customer onboarding time from 4 weeks to 72 hours, improving new customer time-to-value and reducing support tickets by 35%. In the first 6 months of implementation, this translated to $680K in reduced churn."

**Why this works**: Industry identified, company size clear, specific metric with timeframe, business impact quantified in dollars.

### Bad Case Study Introduction
"Our client is a growing tech company. After implementing our solution, they saw significant improvements in their onboarding process and customer satisfaction. Results were impressive."

**Why this fails**: No size/context, no specific metrics, vague language ("significant," "impressive"), no business outcome.

### Good Challenge Section
"The sales engineering team averaged 25 hours per week on manual contract customization—reviewing templates, negotiating terms, and tracking versions across email threads. This consumed 40% of their bandwidth, forcing deals to slip past their 14-day close target. In 2022, this delay cost the company $3.2M in lost quarterly revenue and contributed to 12% sales team turnover."

**Why this works**: Specific time spent (25 hrs/week), percentage of capacity, outcome (14-day target missed), business impact ($3.2M), secondary impact (turnover).

### Bad Challenge Section
"The team had trouble with contracts. This was slowing down sales, and customers were unhappy about the delays."

**Why this fails**: No specifics, no numbers, vague impact, not credible.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Vague Metrics** — "Improved efficiency" or "saved time" without numbers. Always quantify: "Saved 12 hours per week per team member" or "Reduced processing time from 6 days to 8 hours."

2. **Missing the Before-and-After Frame** — Stating only the "after" result. The comparison is what makes results credible. Always show: "Was 72 hours, now 4 hours."

3. **Losing the Human Element** — Case studies become sterile corporate documents. Keep the challenge relatable by showing the impact on teams (burnout, morale, turnover).

4. **Over-Crediting the Solution** — Implying your product alone drove results. A credible case study always includes client effort: "Working with our implementation team, they redesigned workflows, trained staff, and committed executive sponsorship."

5. **Skipping Timeframe Context** — "Increased revenue by 40%" without saying "in 12 months" or "year-over-year." Timeframe matters; it shows sustainability.

6. **Using Generic Client Quotes** — Quotes that could apply to any product ("Great solution, highly recommend"). Demand specificity: quote should reference specific outcomes or implementation details.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **The Feature Dump** — Listing product features instead of showing business outcomes. Anti-pattern: "The platform includes workflow automation, real-time dashboards, and API integrations." Better: "Automated contract routing reduced review time from 6 days to 2 hours, letting sales close deals 5 days faster."

2. **The Fiction** — Exaggerating results or fabricating metrics. This destroys credibility faster than transparency. If you don't have exact numbers, say so: "Approximately 25-30 hours per week" is better than a made-up exact figure.

3. **The One-Size-Fits-All** — Writing a case study so generic it applies to any client. Specificity sells. Name names, cite dates, reference industry-specific challenges (manufacturing throughput, SaaS unit economics, healthcare compliance).

4. **The Missing Link** — No clear connection between implementation and outcome. Prospects need to understand the causal chain: (1) This was broken, (2) We fixed it this way, (3) Here's proof it's better. Don't skip steps.

5. **The Invisible Client** — Case studies written from your perspective ("We implemented," "We discovered"), not the client's. The focus should be on their situation and their success. Use client names and voices liberally.
