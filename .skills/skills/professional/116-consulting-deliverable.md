# Consulting Deliverable

## Purpose
Consulting deliverables translate complex analyses into persuasive, client-ready documents that drive decisions. The pyramid principle (conclusion-first, hierarchical reasoning) combined with MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive) frameworks creates documents that executives trust. This skill ensures each slide advances the argument through a single, actionable insight supported by annotated evidence.

## When to Use
- **Building strategy presentations** (market entry, restructuring, M&A analysis)
- **Executive summaries** for board reviews or investor updates
- **Problem-solving decks** where hypothesis-driven reasoning matters
- **Client handoff materials** requiring polish and citation-level rigor
- **Do NOT use when**: presenting preliminary findings, exploratory workshops, or brainstorm documents (use simpler formats); responding to urgent questions (this demands deliberate crafting)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Define the Hypothesis Chain
Start with a single, testable hypothesis. Break it into 3-5 sub-hypotheses that, if proven, collectively prove the main claim. This is your deck's logical spine.

Example: "We should enter the Asian market" breaks into:
- Market size and growth rate support a 5-year ROI threshold
- Competitive intensity allows margin capture
- Regulatory barriers are surmountable with a partnership strategy
- Our capabilities map to customer needs

### Step 2: Apply MECE Logic to Each Section
For every analysis, ensure categories are mutually exclusive (no overlap) and collectively exhaustive (no gaps).

Market segmentation example (MECE):
- By company size: SMB | Mid-market | Enterprise (exclusive, exhaustive)
- Bad: SMB | Mid-market | High-growth (not exhaustive: where do large mature companies fit?)

For hypothesis-driven decks, MECE also applies to barriers, solutions, and stakeholders.

### Step 3: Develop the Situation-Complication-Resolution Arc
**Situation**: Current state; what the audience knows
**Complication**: The tension or gap between current and desired state
**Resolution**: Your recommendation or insight

Example for a restructuring deck:
- Situation: Margin compression across all divisions (true statement of today)
- Complication: R&D spending hasn't declined proportionally; competitive cost structure is 15% lower
- Resolution: Consolidate back-office, reallocate savings to R&D, target margin recovery by Q3

### Step 4: Create One Insight Per Slide
Each slide must contain one clear insight in the title (e.g., "Consolidating warehouse operations saves $12M annually without service impact"). The slide then provides evidence, not explanation.

**Action Titles** (bad):
- "Revenue by Channel"
- "Customer Feedback"

**Action Titles** (good):
- "Direct channel shows 3x unit economics vs. retail; recommend 40% sales shift by Q2"
- "Customers cite ease-of-use as 1st priority, yet our setup takes 6 hours; simplifying onboarding unlocks $8M upsell"

### Step 5: Annotate All Exhibits
Every chart, table, or data display must include:
- **Source** (right-aligned, small font): "Gartner Market Share, March 2024"
- **Data definition** (below title): "Includes recurring and one-time licenses; excludes services"
- **Key metric callout** (annotation or highlight): Arrows, boxes, or labels pointing to the insight
- **Sample size/confidence note** (if relevant): "Based on survey of 247 customers; ±4% margin of error"

Example bad exhibit: Bar chart showing "Q1, Q2, Q3 revenue" with no labels, sources, or callouts.

Example good exhibit: Bar chart with source (internal sales CRM), annotation highlighting Q2 spike ("32% boost after feature release"), and confidence note ("consolidated from 14 regional offices").

### Step 6: Build the Executive Summary Memo
Before the deck, create a 1-2 page memo:
- **Headline** (boldface): State the core recommendation in one sentence
- **Situation** (1 paragraph): Market, organizational context
- **Complication** (1 paragraph): Why the status quo doesn't work
- **Recommendation** (3-5 bullets): Specific actions with expected outcomes
- **Timeline and dependencies** (bullet list)
- **Next steps**: Who does what by when

This memo often circulates before the full deck and sets expectations.

### Step 7: Structure the Slide Deck Itself
Typical consulting deck sequence:
1. **Cover slide** (client name, date, team, confidentiality)
2. **Table of contents** (frame the sections and story)
3. **Executive summary** (2 slides max: recommendation + top 3 supporting pillars)
4. **Background** (market, organizational context; 1-2 slides)
5. **Problem definition** (situation-complication; 1-2 slides)
6. **Analysis section** (each slide = one insight; typically 8-15 slides)
7. **Recommendations** (3-5 options, each with pros/cons/financial impact; 3-5 slides)
8. **Implementation roadmap** (timeline, resources, milestones; 2-3 slides)
9. **Appendix** (detailed data, case studies, full calculations; as needed)

### Step 8: Calibrate Tone and Confidence Language
Consulting decks use specific language to convey confidence and accountability:
- **Highest confidence**: "We recommend..." / "Our analysis shows..." / "Clients in this cohort achieved..."
- **High confidence**: "The evidence suggests..." / "Based on industry benchmarks..."
- **Medium confidence**: "Early indicators point to..." / "If market conditions hold..."
- **Lower confidence**: "We should test..." / "Explore whether..." / "Scenario analysis indicates..."

Never waver mid-sentence between confidence levels. Match language to data quality.

## Quality Gates

1. **Pyramid Principle Test**: Flip through the deck. Each section title and every slide title should state a conclusion, not a topic. If any slide title is a question or neutral noun, reframe.

2. **MECE Audit**: For any framework or segmentation, verify categories don't overlap and cover all cases. List out the framework and test with examples from your data.

3. **Exhibit Rigor Check**: Every chart has a source, a data definition note, and a key finding annotation. No bare data; all exhibits serve the narrative.

4. **Confidence Calibration**: Scan language. Do confidence statements match data quality? Rank-order conclusions from high to low confidence and verify the language hierarchy.

5. **Single Insight Per Slide**: Read only the slide title (not the content). Does it tell a complete, actionable story? If you need the chart to understand the title, rewrite the title.

6. **Client Handoff Readiness**: Would this deck survive a 360-degree review (peer, client, executive)? Check for typos, consistent branding, clear callouts, proper citations.

## Examples

### Good Consulting Insight

**Slide Title**: "Consolidating 3 customer success teams into 1 shared service saves $800K annually while reducing churn by 2%"

**Chart**: Comparison of churn rates (before/after) and cost structure (headcount + tools), with source and annotations showing which regions benefited most.

**Why it works**: The title delivers the insight (financial + customer impact). The chart backs it with evidence. An executive can decide based on the title alone.

### Bad Consulting Insight

**Slide Title**: "Customer Success Organization Review"

**Chart**: Organizational chart with 47 boxes

**Why it fails**: The title states no conclusion. The chart is exploratory, not explanatory. A reader doesn't know what to think.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Cliff-hanging conclusions**: Building to a recommendation across 20 slides, only stating it on slide 21. Share the headline in your executive summary; use the deck to prove it. Executives stop reading if they don't know where you're going.

2. **Mixed confidence levels**: Using "we recommend" and "suggests" and "might" in the same recommendation section. Pick one confidence register per section. If data quality is mixed, call it out in a dedicated slide ("Confidence Assessment").

3. **Charts without annotation**: Including a complex, multi-series chart with no callouts or color highlights. The reader can't find your insight. Use arrows, boxes, or contrasting colors to guide the eye to the key finding.

4. **Ignoring MECE gaps**: Analyzing customers by "SMB, Mid-Market, Tech Sector" (overlapping categories). Before finalizing your framework, test it with real data. Are there customers who don't fit?

5. **Appendix buried**: Putting critical detail (methodology, data sources, case studies) in a tiny appendix. Decide: Is this insight worth featuring? If yes, include the supporting detail in the main deck or reference a specific appendix slide. Never hide.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **The deck as data dump**: 100-slide appendix showing every analysis performed. Instead, create a supporting document or model spreadsheet; the deck tells the story.

2. **Lack of client alignment**: Presenting a recommendation that contradicts the client's existing strategy or stated priorities without acknowledging the tension. Always address stakeholder concerns explicitly ("We recognize you've invested in X; here's why we recommend Y").

3. **Analysis without recommendation**: Presenting findings without clear next steps. "This is interesting" is not a conclusion. Every section should end with "Therefore, we should..." or "This means you should..."

4. **Inconsistent storytelling across slides**: Recommendation on slide 20 differs from the executive summary. Ensure the memo, deck title page, and final recommendation all align. Review for consistency before presenting.
