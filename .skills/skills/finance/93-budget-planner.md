# Budget Planner

## Purpose

Without a budget, you spend until you run out of money. With a budget, you direct money toward goals. This skill walks you through categorizing income and expenses, setting realistic savings targets, tracking actual vs. budgeted spending, and making adjustments when reality diverges. You'll exit with a monthly/annual budget that's realistic, actionable, and drives you toward financial goals.

## When to Use

- You want to understand where your money goes
- You're trying to save for a goal (house, car, sabbatical, retirement)
- Your spending exceeds your income or you're unsure why
- You're about to make a major financial change (salary cut, new expense, debt payoff)
- You want to reduce stress around money
- **Do NOT use when**: You have extremely variable income (freelance with no runway) or you're in financial crisis (seek professional advice first)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Determine Your Income (After-Tax, Net)

Budget against money you actually keep, not gross income.

**Calculate net monthly income**:

```
Gross Monthly Income:           $6,000
Taxes (federal, state, FICA):   -$1,500  (assume 25%)
Retirement contributions:       -$400    (401k, if pre-tax)
Health insurance premiums:      -$200    (if pre-tax)
Other deductions:               -$100
Net Monthly Income:             $3,800
```

Use net (take-home) income, not gross. If you're self-employed or have variable income, use a conservative estimate (e.g., last 12-month average or lower).

**For variable income**:
- Calculate last 12 months of income
- Divide by 12 to get average
- Use 80% of average as your budget baseline (conservative buffer)
- Put overage (months above 80% baseline) into a "variable income buffer" account

**Example**: Last 12 months averaged $5,000/month. Budget on $4,000/month. Months where you earn $5,000+ go to buffer.

### Step 2: Categorize Your Expenses

Group all expenses into categories. You'll assign a budget to each.

**Standard expense categories**:

**Fixed Expenses** (same every month):
- Rent/Mortgage: $1,500
- Car payment: $300
- Insurance (auto, health, etc.): $200
- Utilities (power, water, internet): $150
- Subscriptions (streaming, software, gym): $50
- **Total Fixed**: $2,200

**Variable Expenses** (fluctuate):
- Groceries: $400
- Dining out / entertainment: $200
- Gas / transportation: $150
- Clothing: $100
- Household items: $75
- Personal care: $50
- **Total Variable**: $975

**Discretionary** (fun, flexible):
- Hobbies: $100
- Travel / vacations: $150
- Gifts: $75
- Entertainment: $100
- **Total Discretionary**: $425

**Debt Repayment** (credit cards, loans):
- Credit card payments: $200
- Student loans: $150
- **Total Debt**: $350

**Savings & Goals**:
- Emergency fund: $200
- Retirement (after-tax): $150
- Vacation fund: $100
- **Total Savings**: $450

**Sum all categories**: $2,200 + $975 + $425 + $350 + $450 = $4,400

**Compare to income**: Net income is $3,800, budget is $4,400. You're $600 over. Adjust now.

### Step 3: Align Budget to Income (Make Cuts or Adjust)

If expenses exceed income, you have three options:

**Option A: Increase Income**
- Ask for raise, start side gig, sell unused items
- Most aspirational; not always possible in short term

**Option B: Cut Expenses**
- Reduce discretionary (dining out, entertainment): $425 → $200 saves $225
- Reduce variable (dining out, clothes): $975 → $750 saves $225
- Cancel subscriptions, downsize car, relocate to cheaper housing
- Cut $600 to match income

**Option C: Reduce Savings Goals**
- Cut emergency fund savings: $200 → $0
- Cut extra retirement: $150 → $0
- Cut vacation fund: $100 → $0
- Saves $450, still $150 short, cut discretionary by $150

**Realistic approach**: Combination of B and C. Cut discretionary/variable by $350, reduce extra savings by $250. Now budget = $4,050, within $3,800 income (with $250 deficit to close with savings buffers).

**Adjusted budget**:
```
Fixed:        $2,200
Variable:     $825 (reduced from $975)
Discretionary:$200 (reduced from $425)
Debt:         $350
Savings:      $225 (reduced from $450)
TOTAL:        $3,800 (matches income)
```

This is tight but realistic. You've cut wants, maintained debt payoff, and saved a bit.

### Step 4: Set Savings Goals and Targets

Where is that $225/month savings going?

**Priority order** (common framework):
1. **Emergency fund**: 3-6 months of expenses in liquid savings
2. **High-interest debt**: Credit cards, payday loans (>12% interest)
3. **Retirement**: 401k, Roth IRA, compound growth
4. **Intermediate goals**: House down payment, car replacement (2-10 years)
5. **Discretionary goals**: Travel, hobbies (nice to have)

**Example allocation of $225 savings**:
- Emergency fund (until 3 months expenses, ~$10,000): $150/month
- Retirement (after-tax IRA): $75/month

Once emergency fund is full (~67 months or 5.5 years), redirect that $150 elsewhere.

### Step 6: Track Actual Spending vs. Budget

Budgeting without tracking is just wishful thinking. Track actual spending.

**Weekly quick check** (10 min):
- Review credit card and bank transactions
- Compare to budget categories
- Note any "surprises" (overspending in a category)

**Monthly deep review** (30 min):
- Sum actual spending by category
- Compare to budget
- Calculate variance: Actual - Budget

**Example**:
```
CATEGORY        BUDGET   ACTUAL   VARIANCE
Rent            $1,500   $1,500   $0
Groceries         $400     $450   +$50 (over)
Dining out        $150     $220   +$70 (over)
Gas               $100      $95   -$5 (under)
Entertainment     $100      $140   +$40 (over)
Credit card       $200      $200   $0
Emergency fund    $150      $150   $0
TOTAL           $3,700   $3,755   +$55 (over)
```

**Variance analysis**:
- Dining out and entertainment over by $110 combined
- Groceries over by $50 (food costs rose? bought extras?)
- Gas under by $5 (not significant)
- **Overall**: $55 over budget, covered by your $100 buffer

### Step 7: Make Adjustments

If variance is consistent (over budget every month), adjust the budget.

**Three types of adjustments**:

**A. One-time adjustments** (unexpected event):
- "March has car insurance payment, which doesn't happen every month"
- Solution: Spread that cost across the year, OR reduce another category that month

**B. Recurring adjustments** (pattern observed):
- "Dining out is always $220, never $150"
- Solution: Update budget from $150 to $220; cut something else to compensate

**C. Spending discipline** (you're just overspending):
- "Entertainment was $140 this month, $160 last month. Budget is $100 but I don't want to cut it."
- Solution: Accept it costs $140-150/month, reduce something else, or increase income

**After adjustment**, your budget is more realistic and more likely to be followed.

### Step 8: Build an Annual Budget and Forecast

Monthly budgets show the recurring picture. Annual budgets capture one-time expenses and seasonal variation.

```
ANNUAL BUDGET 2026

Monthly Average:        $3,700/month × 12 = $44,400

One-time / Irregular Expenses:
  Car maintenance:      $500 (every 3 months = $2,000/year)
  Car insurance:        $150/month × 12 = $1,800 (included in monthly avg)
  Annual vacation:      $2,000 (July or August)
  Gifts (holidays):     $600 (December)
  Medical (dental, etc): $500 (annual checkups, copays)

Adjusted Annual Total: $44,400 + $2,000 + $600 + $500 = $47,500

Annual Income: $3,800 × 12 = $45,600

Annual Deficit: $47,500 - $45,600 = -$1,900

What's missing? You're short $1,900/year. Options:
- Increase side gig income by $160/month
- Reduce annual vacation budget by $1,900 (skip vacation this year)
- Dip into savings for one-time items
- Combination of above

Most realistic: Increase side gig by $100/month ($1,200/year) + reduce vacation by $700.
```

This annual view surfaces gaps monthly budgets can hide.

Create a forecast for the next 12 months showing income, expenses, and projected cash balance.

```

May      $3,800    $3,700      $100       $10,000
Jun      $3,800    $3,700      $100       $10,100
Jul      $3,800    $6,200    -$2,400      $7,700 (vacation month)
Aug      $3,800    $3,700      $100       $7,800
Sep      $3,800    $3,700      $100       $7,900
Oct      $3,800    $3,700      $100       $8,000

```

This shows: You'll dip to $7,500 by year-end if you take a vacation in July. Is that acceptable? If not, adjust vacation budget or increase income.

### Step 10: Review and Adjust Quarterly

**Quarterly review** (1 hour):
- Compare YTD actual to YTD budget
- Are you on track?
- What's changed (income, unexpected expenses, lifestyle)?
- Update next quarter's budget

**Annual review** (2 hours):
- Full year actual vs. budget
- Major variances: why?
- Goals: Did you save enough for emergency fund, retirement, goals?
- Next year's budget: Based on what actually happened, not what you hoped

## Quality Gates (5+)

1. **Income Accurate**: Is your net income realistic, not hopeful? (Use actual take-home from last 3 paychecks.)
2. **Expenses Sum to Income**: Do fixed + variable + savings equal income? (If not, adjust or increase income.)
3. **Savings Prioritized**: Are you building emergency fund before discretionary? (Yes = healthy financial foundation.)
4. **Tracking Happening**: Are you reviewing actual vs. budget monthly? (Without tracking, budget is fiction.)
5. **Variance Addressed**: When you overspend a category, do you adjust or identify the cause? (Yes = learning and control.)
6. **Annual Aligned**: Does annual budget match 12x monthly, plus one-time items?

## Examples

### Good Budget (Realistic, Tracked, Adjusted)

**Monthly budget**: Income $3,800, fixed $2,200, variable $825, discretionary $200, debt $350, savings $225

**January actual**: Spending $3,755 (dining out and entertainment higher than budget)

**Adjustment**: Increase dining out budget from $150 to $200, reduce discretionary shopping from $100 to $50

**Result**: Spending aligns with reality, budget becomes predictive, savings goal stays intact

### Bad Budget (Unrealistic, Untracked)

**"I'll spend $2,000/month and save $1,800"** (on $3,800 income)

Problems:
- No detail on what $2,000 covers
- Saving 47% of income is unrealistic without sacrifice details
- Never tracked; actual spending is probably $3,500+
- No variance analysis; overspend every month, feel like a failure

## Common Mistakes (3+)

1. **Gross vs. Net**: You budget on $5,000 gross, but net is $3,800. Overspend immediately. Budget on take-home, always.

2. **Category Inflation**: You budget $100 for dining out but it's really $200. Don't hide the number; adjust budget or cut spending. Honesty feeds control.

3. **No Seasonal Adjustment**: Budget is same every month, but January has car insurance, July has vacation, December has gifts. Annual forecast surfaces seasonality.

4. **Savings Last Priority**: You budget "whatever's left" for savings. That's usually $0. Budget savings first (pay yourself first), then discretionary. Reverse the order.

5. **Emergency Fund Neglected**: You have $0 emergency savings and budget toward vacation. First, build 3-month emergency fund, then optimize. Emergency fund = insurance.

6. **Budget Set and Forgot**: You build a budget, follow it for 1 month, then ignore it. Review monthly. Adjust quarterly. Budget is a living tool, not a one-time exercise.

## Anti-Patterns (3+)

1. **Budgeting Perfectionism**: You spend 8 hours perfecting every category, then abandon budget because it's rigid. Build a 80% accurate budget in 90 minutes, refine from real data.

2. **Shame Spiral**: You overspend and feel guilty, so you avoid looking at budget. Budget isn't judgment; it's awareness. Overspend, understand why, adjust. No shame.

3. **Lifestyle Creep Ignored**: You get a raise, feel richer, spending increases automatically. Now your $4,000/month budget is $5,000. Next raise, same thing. Every raise, allocate 50% to savings, 50% to increased lifestyle.

4. **One-Time Items Treated as Recurring**: Car repair costs $800; you add "$800/month car maintenance" to budget. No. It's one-time. Track separately and adjust annual forecast.
