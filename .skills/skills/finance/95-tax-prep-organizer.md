# Tax Prep Organizer

## Purpose

Tax season stress comes from scrambling for documents and forgetting deductions. This skill provides a system: income sources to track, deduction categories, a document checklist, a deadline timeline, and an organization process. You'll exit with a folder (physical or digital) of organized documents, a list of deductions you can claim, and a timeline that prevents last-minute panic.

**Legal disclaimer**: This is organizational guidance, not tax advice. For tax strategy (how to structure income, what deductions are allowable), consult a CPA or tax professional.

## When to Use

- You're self-employed or have 1099 income
- You're preparing for annual tax filing
- You want to track deductions all year, not scramble at tax time
- You're unsure what's deductible
- You want to reduce tax-filing stress and errors
- **Do NOT use when**: You're in tax trouble (audit, back taxes, penalties—consult a tax professional first)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Determine Your Filing Status and Income Sources

**Filing status** (single, married filing jointly, head of household):
- Determines tax bracket and standard deduction
- Affects deductibility of certain expenses
- Must be correct before filing

**Income sources** (all places money comes from):

**W-2 Income** (employment):
- Salary from employer
- Bonus
- Employer provides W-2 form (by Jan 31)

**1099 Income** (self-employment, freelance, consulting):
- Client payments
- You must track this; client reports on 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC
- You're responsible for self-employment tax (15.3% for Social Security + Medicare)

**Investment Income**:
- Dividends and interest: 1099-INT, 1099-DIV
- Capital gains from selling stocks: You track cost basis, sale price
- Rental income: 1099-NEC if paid by service platform, or you track directly

**Other**:
- Gig work (Uber, TaskRabbit): Track yourself; may get 1099-NEC
- Cryptocurrency gains: Tracked by exchanges (1099-K)
- Gambling winnings: Track; fully taxable

**Why this matters**: Different income types have different tax treatment and documentation requirements. List all your sources now.

### Step 2: Build Your Deduction Categories

Deductions reduce taxable income (you only pay tax on income minus deductions).

**Standard Deduction vs. Itemized**:
- **Standard deduction**: Fixed amount you can deduct (2026: ~$14,500 single, ~$29,000 married)
- **Itemized deductions**: Sum of specific expenses (mortgage interest, property tax, medical, charitable)
- You choose whichever is higher. If standard deduction is higher (most people), use it.

**If you're self-employed**, you must itemize many business expenses:

**Home Office** (if you work from home):
- Percentage of rent/mortgage, utilities, internet
- Calculation: (Home office sq ft / Total sq ft) × monthly rent
- Or simplified: $5/sq ft, up to 300 sq ft = up to $1,500/year
- Example: 200 sq ft home office × $5 = $1,000 deduction

**Business Expenses**:
- Office supplies: $200
- Software/subscriptions: $500 (Adobe, Slack, Notion, etc.)
- Equipment: Laptop, monitor, desk (if used for business)
- Internet: Percentage of bill used for business
- Phone: Percentage used for business (70% if mostly work)

**Travel**:
- Client meetings: Transportation, mileage (IRS rate: $0.67/mile in 2026)
- Conferences: Registration, flight, hotel (if directly related to business)
- Non-deductible: Commute to office, vacation mixed with work

**Meals & Entertainment**:
- Client lunches: 50% deductible (company pays for lunch, you deduct half)
- Solo meals while traveling for work: 50% deductible
- NOT deductible: Personal meals, unless traveling for business

**Professional Services**:
- Lawyer, accountant fees: Fully deductible
- Marketing consultant: Deductible
- Coaching / classes for skill development: Deductible

**Vehicles**:
- Mileage: Track miles driven for business, deduct at IRS rate
- OR actual expenses: Insurance, gas, maintenance (calculate % business use)
- Commute: Not deductible
- Client visits: Deductible

**Health Insurance** (Self-employed):
- Self-employed health insurance deduction: 100% of premiums paid
- Reduces taxable income before self-employment tax (saves ~15%)

**Retirement Contributions**:
- SEP-IRA: Up to 20% of net self-employment income, capped at $70k/year (2026)
- Solo 401k: Up to $70k/year (employee) + employer contributions
- Reduces taxable income

**Example deduction total**:
```
Self-employed Income:                   $80,000
Home office:                            -$1,000
Office supplies:                          -$500
Software:                                 -$600
Internet (50% business use):              -$300
Mileage (4,000 miles × $0.67):          -$2,680
Health insurance:                       -$6,000
SEP-IRA (20%):                         -$14,400
TOTAL DEDUCTIONS:                      -$25,480

Taxable Income:                         $54,520
```

### Step 3: Create a Document Checklist

Before tax time, gather all documents you'll need.

**W-2 Employees Need**:
- [ ] W-2 from employer(s) (by Jan 31)
- [ ] 1099 forms if you have side gigs (by Jan 31)
- [ ] Brokerage statements if you sold stocks (1099-B)
- [ ] Interest/dividend statements (1099-INT, 1099-DIV)
- [ ] Mortgage interest statement (1098, if you itemize)
- [ ] Property tax paid documentation (city/county records)
- [ ] Charitable donations (receipts, bank statements)
- [ ] Medical expense receipts (if you exceed 7.5% of AGI)

**Self-Employed Need**:
- [ ] 1099 forms from clients (by Jan 31) - you also track own records
- [ ] Income ledger: All client payments received (spreadsheet or accounting software)
- [ ] Expense receipts: Home office, supplies, software, travel (organize by category)
- [ ] Mileage log: Date, miles, purpose for business drives (start Jan 1, track daily)
- [ ] Business bank account statements (shows income and expenses)
- [ ] Credit card statement (if business expenses on personal card)
- [ ] Health insurance premium invoices (if self-employed)
- [ ] Retirement contribution records (proof of SEP-IRA or Solo 401k deposits)
- [ ] Schedule C worksheet (self-employment income and deductions)

**Everyone Should Have**:
- [ ] Social Security number or ITIN
- [ ] Prior year tax return (for reference)
- [ ] Photo ID
- [ ] Bank account (for direct deposit refund, if applicable)

### Step 4: Set Up Year-Round Tracking

Don't scramble in March. Track all year.

**Weekly** (15 min):
- Log any business expenses (receipt, amount, category)
- Track mileage if driving for business (date, miles, purpose)
- Record any client payments received

**Monthly** (30 min):
- Review bank/credit card statements
- File receipts by category (supplies, travel, meals, etc.)
- Reconcile income ledger to bank deposits

**Quarterly** (1 hour):
- Sum income and expenses by category
- Check if you owe estimated quarterly taxes (self-employed)
- Identify any deductions you've missed

**Annually** (before year-end, 2 hours):
- Review total income and deductions
- Estimate tax liability
- Plan any last-minute deductions (e.g., charitable donations before Dec 31)
- Make final retirement contributions (deadline is April 15 for prior year)

### Step 5: Organize Documents

**Physical organization** (if you keep receipts):
- Get an accordion file or binder
- Create sections: Income, Home Office, Supplies, Travel, Meals, Health Insurance, Retirement
- File receipts in labeled envelopes or sheet protectors
- Keep all in one place so you can grab it for tax time or audit

**Digital organization** (better option):
- Create a folder structure on Google Drive, Dropbox, or OneDrive:
  ```
  2026 Tax Documents/
    Income/
      - 1099s
      - W-2s
      - Income ledger
    Deductions/
      - Home office
      - Supplies (screenshots or PDF of receipts)
      - Travel (flights, hotels, mileage log)
      - Meals
    Bank statements/
      - Business bank (monthly PDFs)
      - Business credit card (monthly PDFs)
    Receipts/
      - Scanned or photos of receipts
  ```
- Use an expense tracking app (Wave, FreshBooks, or QuickBooks) that automatically categorizes
- Upload receipts as you go (use phone camera or receipt scanner app)

**Backup**:
- Keep originals for 7 years (IRS audit window)
- Scan important documents (backup to cloud, in case of loss)

### Step 6: Create Your Tax Timeline

Mark these dates in your calendar so nothing sneaks up on you.

```
JANUARY
- Jan 31: Receive W-2s and 1099s from clients/employers
- Jan 31: Year-end financial review and reconciliation

FEBRUARY
- Feb 1-15: Gather remaining documents, organize by category
- Feb 15: Deadline for making prior-year SEP-IRA or Solo 401k contributions
- Feb 28: File taxes if you're not getting a refund (to avoid interest)

MARCH
- March 1-31: Meet with CPA/tax prep software to file
- March 31: Final deadline for most business tax filings (not individual 1040)

APRIL
- April 15: Individual tax deadline (1040)
- April 15: Deadline for SEP-IRA/Solo 401k contributions for prior year (last chance)
- April 30: Quarterly estimated tax payment due (if self-employed)

JULY
- July 15: Quarterly estimated tax payment due

SEPTEMBER
- Sept 15: Quarterly estimated tax payment due

DECEMBER
- Dec 15: Quarterly estimated tax payment due
- Dec 31: Last day to make charitable contributions (if you want to deduct this year)
- Dec 31: Last day to make certain retirement contributions
```

### Step 7: Estimate Your Tax Liability

Before filing, estimate what you'll owe so you're not surprised.

**Simple estimation** (if you have a CPA, ask them):

```
Gross Income:                          $80,000
Less: Deductions (home office, etc.): -$25,000
Taxable Income:                        $55,000

Federal tax (2026 rates, ~22%):       -$12,100
Self-employment tax (15.3%):          -$12,300  (on self-employment income)
State tax (varies):                    -$2,000   (estimate)

Total estimated tax:                  -$26,400

Less: Taxes withheld from W-2 jobs:   +$5,000   (if you have one)
Less: Estimated tax payments made:    +$6,000   (if you paid quarterly)

NET DUE / REFUND:                     -$15,400  (likely owe this amount)
```

This is rough, but it tells you: "I'll likely owe ~$15,400" so you're not shocked on April 15.

### Step 8: Choose Your Tax Filing Method

**Option A: DIY Software** ($0-250):
- TurboTax, H&R Block, TaxAct
- Good for: Simple returns, W-2 only, some 1099
- Cost: $60-250 depending on complexity
- Time: 2-4 hours

**Option B: CPA or Tax Professional** ($300-1,000+):
- Accountant prepares your return
- Good for: Self-employed, complex deductions, multiple income sources
- Cost: $300-1,000 (or percentage of refund)
- Time: You organize docs, they file

**Option C: Hybrid**:
- You use software for initial draft
- CPA reviews and files
- Cost: ~$500
- Good balance of control and professional eyes

**My recommendation**: If you're self-employed or have complex deductions, hire a CPA (year 1). They'll optimize and educate you. Years 2+, you can do it yourself or use their guidance.

### Step 9: Prepare for Submission

**Before you file** (via software or CPA):

- [ ] All 1099s received and verified against your records
- [ ] Income total matches your ledger
- [ ] Deductions organized by category
- [ ] Mileage log completed (if claiming miles)
- [ ] Receipts backed up / scanned
- [ ] Estimated tax payments and W-2 withholding noted
- [ ] Address and legal name correct
- [ ] Bank account info for direct deposit (if getting refund)

**If filing with software**:
- Go through form line-by-line
- Cross-check numbers against 1099s and your records
- Review tax summary to ensure it's reasonable
- File electronically (faster, safer than mail)

**If filing with CPA**:
- Give them organized documents
- Be available to answer questions
- Review return before they file
- Ask: "What deductions did we miss? What should I track differently next year?"

**Feb 1**: All documents in Google Drive folder, organized by category, backed up

**Feb 15**: Meets with CPA, gives her the folder

**March 1**: CPA identifies additional deductions: business meals $600, software $200

### Bad Tax Prep (Scrambling, Disorganized, Missed Deductions)

"I'll gather everything in March and file by April 15"

**March 15**: Start looking for receipts, half are missing
**April 1**: Find receipts in shoe box; can't remember which are business
**April 14**: Hire CPA in panic; she's booked, can't take you
**April 15**: Use software, miss obvious deductions (home office, mileage)
**May 15**: CPA finally has availability; too late to amend easily
**Result**: Overpaid taxes, paid rush fee for late filing, regret

## Common Mistakes (3+)

1. **Lost Receipts**: You spend money on business expenses but don't keep receipts. Come tax time, no proof. Rule: Take photo of receipt immediately; back up to cloud.

2. **Mixed Personal/Business**: Gas for commute vs. business trip, personal lunch vs. client lunch. Maintain a mileage log (date, miles, business purpose) to separate them.

3. **Missed Deductions**: You itemize supplies, but forget home office, software, and health insurance. Create a checklist before tax season; don't rely on memory.

4. **Late Estimated Taxes**: As a freelancer, you owe quarterly estimated taxes (April, July, Oct, Jan). Missing these = penalties. Set calendar reminders.

5. **No Tracking Year-Round**: You track everything haphazardly, then in February try to reconstruct. Disaster. Create a simple spreadsheet and log expenses weekly.

6. **Wrong Filing Status**: You got married mid-year and didn't update. Creates errors. Determine your status by Dec 31 of the tax year.

## Anti-Patterns (3+)

1. **Over-Claiming Deductions**: You stretch what's deductible (personal lunch as "client meal," entire vacation as business travel). IRS audits businesses with outlier deductions. Claim what's legitimately deductible; don't cheat.

2. **Trusting Memory**: "I'll remember what I spent in July." You won't. Track in real-time or digitally.

3. **Delaying CPA Meeting**: You plan to meet CPA in April, but she's booked. Meet by mid-February so she has time and can answer optimization questions.
